# The Match Bar Group Bartender's Manual

**Author:** Kevin Armstrong (Group Bars Manager / Group Head Bartender), with contributions from Dale DeGroff, JD, Sasha Petraske, and other Match Bar Group senior staff
**Edition referenced:** 2nd March 08 Draft V6
**Scope:** Internal operations, training and standards manual for The Match Bar Group — a London bar group whose sites include Match EC1, Match Bar (W1), Sosho, The Player, Milk & Honey, Trailer Happiness and Harlem.

## Structural overview

The manual runs to 35 numbered sections (roughly 250 printed pages) organized into five broad movements:

1. **Ethos & service philosophy** (§1–4): Introduction; "The Zen of Bartending" (Dale DeGroff on service); Drinks ethos; Service standards including JD's "20 commandments."
2. **Organisational structure & career ladder** (§5–9): Bar Back → Junior Bartender → Bartender → Senior Bartender job profiles, pay bands, and requirements; Head Bartender/Manager notes; Training (First Tuesday program); Safety; Personal presentation/hygiene.
3. **Craft fundamentals** (§10–19): Equipment and tool usage; Glassware; Drink-creation techniques (shaking, stirring, building, rolling, throwing); Free pouring vs. jiggering; Tasting; Practical/skill requirements (economy of motion, formulation of the round); "The next level" (balance, dilution, presentation, back-bar design); Ice; Basic ingredients — fruit, then liquids/juices/premixes.
4. **Standards & recipes** (§20–23): Standards (station set-up, opening/closing checklists, par levels, bill service, cleaning rotas); Useful shift information; **Dale's 120 classics** (alphabetical classic-cocktail reference); **Match Bar Group Recipes & Originals** (house signature drinks).
5. **Category knowledge & appendices** (§24–35): Beer; Wine; Champagne & sparkling wine; Site specifics (addresses/timelines for each bar); The Law; How to win drinks competitions; Bars we respect; Match facts; Alumni; Glossary; Appendix (shot-count sheet).

## 1. Ethos and service philosophy

The manual opens with an H.L. Mencken quote (1911) elevating bartending to a dignified trade, then Dale DeGroff's essay "The Zen of Bartending," which frames the bar (unlike the dining room) as **shared space that the bartender, not the guest, sets the tone for** — guests get latitude a server in a restaurant would never extend them, and the bartender's job is to read mood, offer discreet theatre (e.g. flaming citrus), and "turn difficult guests into friends."

**JD's "three most important qualities"**: Knowledge, Knowledge, and Personality — in that order; personality is worthless without knowledge.

**JD's 20 commandments** (condensed): make eye contact; offer a cocktail menu; put down coasters (signals "this guest is being served"); offer a sincere greeting; know everything about the product range; upsell knowledgeably (never fake enthusiasm); be efficient (take order → make drinks → sell drinks, never chat mid-prep); be organised (juggle 2 orders, not more); be technically proficient; prioritize a round (long iced drinks first, martinis last, since they warm/deteriorate fastest); secure payment immediately; check back on guests; be thoughtful/proactive (pour wine for guests rather than let them spill); offer food; keep the bar clean (always handle glasses by the base); work as a team; offer a sincere farewell; be as well-presented as the drinks; never serve the drunk/unruly/underage; be helpful, cool and take pride.

**Cutting off a guest** (§4.10) — an escalation ladder: offer water/cool-down time → "busy, busy, busy" avoidance technique → enlist a soberer friend (empower them with free water) → propose food/sit-down → suggest 5 minutes outside → call the manager → security (SIA-certified only) as last resort → police if needed. Staff must never lose their temper, and any decision by a manager must be backed by the whole team even if disputed.

**Dispense service** (§4.11): the strongest/fastest bartender should run dispense (not the weakest, as many groups do), and pre-mixing high-volume drinks (e.g. 20 Cosmopolitans) for dispense-only service is acceptable even though it wouldn't be for guests watching at the bar.

## 2. Career ladder and training

Each level (Bar Back, Junior Bartender, Bartender, Senior Bartender) has a full job profile: department, line manager, salary band (era-specific, e.g. Bar Back £5.52–£6/hr rising to Senior Bartender £8–£8.50/hr), general responsibilities (presentable, polite, uphold standards, protect equipment, dedication to training) and role-specific duties that scale in depth — e.g. a Bar Back must know ingredient locations and juice/syrup recipes but not full cocktail specs; a Bartender must be "fully proficient" in all recipes and able to mentor Juniors.

**Becoming a Senior Bartender** is an 8–12 month process requiring, among other things:
- A recitable company-ethos statement
- A written Senior Bartender exam (ethos, spirits production, cocktails, wine, beer, champagne, history)
- A practical exam (round production, both-hand pouring, economy of motion, cleanliness, final drink quality)
- 3 months of pour-testing at **≥90% accuracy** (95% required for Head Bartenders)
- 2 full weeks working at Sosho and 2 full weeks at Milk & Honey (to absorb Sasha Petraske's dilution/ice methodology)
- 4 weeks of GP (stock/budget) training plus a GP exam
- 1 week cash-up training
- Attendance at Dale DeGroff's introductory seminar
- 7 of 8 "First Tuesday" sessions with a >60% pass rate
- Site-specific operational knowledge and a final interview with JD
- Optional add-ons: WSET Spirits/Wine, licensing course, first aid, an individual research project

**First Tuesday program** (§7): the core training institution — a compulsory, 2-hour (3–5pm) monthly session at The Player, one drinks category per month (e.g. Tequila with Tom Estes, Bourbon with Mark Ridgewell), no topic repeated within a year, 11 sessions/year (December reserved for the end-of-year exam). Each session opens with a test on **last month's** material (forcing spaced repetition), followed by a guided tasting. Lateness = no admission; persistent lateness = disciplinary action. Sample exam questions are reproduced at Entry, Bartender, and Senior Bartender difficulty (e.g. "Name the 6 Classic Malts of Scotland," "How do you make a Mary Pickford?", "What is 'Black Tot Day'?").

**Safety (§8)**: knife discipline; the flat-top bar spoon's welded end is flagged as a genuine injury risk if used to muddle (two documented hand-piercing incidents — use a muddler); Boston glass tempered glass can still fracture under muddling pressure; broken glass must never enter regular bins; any hint of glass or blood contamination voids an entire ice well immediately, no exceptions.

## 3. Equipment and technique

### Core tools (§10)
- **Boston glass** (16oz tapered mixing glass): the primary vessel. Technique notes — build the "eye line" (learn how full the glass needs to be for each glass size) by practicing with water; never ice the glass first (distorts eye line, starts diluting while you're distracted); add cheapest ingredients first (limits loss on a mistake).
- **Boston can** (~30oz): forms the seal with the glass ("Boston shaker"/shaker set); can be "snapped" directly onto an ice well as a scoop-and-seal shortcut; doubles as a small-batch syrup vessel; the "smelly can" fix is a shake of lemon juice, water and a wedge with ice.
- **Toby shaker**: smaller (~16oz), used for one-handed shaking and cap-shaking in Catalina/sling glasses; over-shakes easily.
- **Cobbler (3-piece) shaker**: not used at Match, but common at Milk & Honey; built-in strainer.
- **Flat-top bar spoon** (Bonzer): stirring, layering (pour down the twisted stem over the disc), a ~5ml ladle measure — **never** for muddling (safety risk).
- **Red-top bar spoon**: alternative with more flex, usable for hand-cracking ice.
- **Hawthorn strainer**: used only with the Boston can; spring tension is adjustable via a small metal tab for a tighter/looser pour.
- **Julep strainer**: for stirred/straight-liquor drinks poured from the Boston glass; historically was drunk *through*, not just used to strain.
- **Tea/fine strainer**: used sparingly — only when Hawthorn/Julep can't catch seeds or poor ice; removes the aerated bubbles that make a freshly shaken drink "feel alive" on the first sip (a deliberate trade-off, not a default step).
- **Muddler**: used to release flavor that shaking/hand-squeezing can't (e.g. limes in a Caipirinha); avoid over-bashing citrus skins (releases bitter oils).
- **Canelle knife / citrus zester**: rind extraction for garnish, e.g. citrus knots.
- **Speed pourer** (Spill Stop brand preferred): only fitted to bottles used daily or on the cocktail menu; replace when rusted (taints product) or leaking.
- **Lewis/Louis bag**: canvas bag + muddler/rolling pin for dry, slow-melting crushed ice (Mint Juleps, Mojitos).
- Standard straw/stirrer set: 6" sip straw, 8"/11" jumbo clear straws, 6" plastic ball stirrer (Ascot Wholesale) — no bendy or coloured straws except M&H's metal straws.

### Glassware (§11)
17 house glass types listed (Inverness/Endeavour rocks small/large, Catalina tall cooler ["dangerous"], Squall/Hurricane, Signature martini/Elegance Coupette, Kentucky martini, Signature wine/flute, Islandi/American whiskey shot, Trigger/Icon Tankard, Brandy Balloon, Weiss goblet, Volcano bowl). Rules: match glass shape/volume to the recipe (adjust the recipe, not the glass, when they mismatch); always serve at correct temperature (frozen for stirred/no-ice drinks, chilled otherwise); stock glassware proportionally to actual usage (mostly highballs, not martini glasses); handle stemware by the base — most breakage happens during polishing.

### Drink-creation techniques (§12)
- **Shaking**: as much ice as possible, firmly, **7–10 seconds** (standardized to the house Hoshizaki ice cube). Grip the shaker so the Boston can's seam faces the customer (so a failed seal sprays the bartender, not the guest). Never let a shaken drink sit — serve immediately. Technique for separating the can/glass: left hand grips the straight seam, right palm strikes at ~135° from the index finger, sharply.
- **Cap shaking**: quick technique using the can/Toby shaker as a lid over a glass already built — house example is the Match Spring Punch.
- **Stirring**: **15–20 seconds** in a fully-iced Boston glass, red-top spoon preferred, motion from the wrist only; hold the glass by its base (not the bowl) to avoid warming it; a Thermos or wine cooler used as the mixing vessel was measured (Bar 05 "Soulshakers" test) to produce Martinis **3–5°F colder** than a standard Boston glass.
- **Rule of thumb**: any drink with fresh juice is shaken; pure liquor/liqueur drinks are stirred; **exception — Bloody Mary/tomato-juice drinks are rolled, never shaken** (shaking breaks tomato juice's molecular structure and thins its texture).
- **Building**: ingredients mixed directly in the serve glass (highballs like Dark & Stormy); never build a cocktail directly into a martini glass.
- **Rolling**: gentle wave-motion transfer between two vessels — used for Bloody Marys and other texture-sensitive juices.
- **Throwing**: aerating a stirred-style drink (Martini, Manhattan) by pouring between two Hawthorn-capped cans held at arm's length, waterfall-style.
- **Blue Blazer**: flaming spirit poured between two long-handled heatproof vessels — flagged as high-risk showmanship, "you almost certainly will never need it."

### Free pouring vs. jiggering (§13)
House rule: if unsure of any recipe, always jigger first to learn the taste, then graduate to free pouring. Jiggering is actually faster than two-handed free pouring only when using one hand or pouring from a jug/unspouted bottle. The house teaches **counting to 5 for a 25ml pour** (so every count = 5ml, letting a 7-count = 35ml etc.) rather than the more common 4-count, because it's evenly divisible. All staff are expected to master **two-handed free pouring** and must pass a **free-pour exam** to progress levels; a "cut" (how you end a pour) should be understated and graceful, not showy — the pop/bounce pour (a quick downward jerk that creates a temporary air lock) is named as the most effective way to stop a pour cleanly.

### Practical skills (§15)
- **Economy of motion**: batch repeated ingredients across a round (e.g. handle lime juice once for 3 drinks, not three times).
- **Knowing the specs / "the window"**: house recipes are built to the widest common denominator of taste — not too sweet/sour/strong — so a drink is unlikely to be sent back while still showcasing the base spirit.
- **Formulation of the round**: sequence drink production so everything finishes together, accounting for how fast each drink deteriorates (iced long drinks first, martinis/stirred drinks last, served immediately since they're "the most volatile").
- Presentation should never slip under pressure — quoting Dale DeGroff: "I say there's always time" for garnish.

## 4. Balance, dilution and ice (the house "theory")

**Kevin on Balance** (§16.2) — the manual's clearest statement of craft philosophy, using the **Daiquiri** as the reference case:

> House Daiquiri spec: **50ml Havana Blanco : 15ml fresh lime juice : 15ml simple syrup**

A 5ml swing in lime vs. sugar (10ml relative deficit) is enough to tip a "harmonious" drink into an unpleasantly sour one — and most customers can't articulate why a drink is off, they just won't reorder it (sometimes for 6 months). Because the house sugar syrup is made 1:1 (see below), it balances almost exactly with equal parts fresh lemon/lime — simplifying recipe design versus higher-brix commercial syrups. Rules of thumb given: total sweet+sour should never exceed the spirit volume (50ml spirit balances with roughly 10–25ml sour + 10–25ml sweet, more than that and strength disappears); non-simple-syrup sweeteners (honey, agave, Triple Sec) don't balance 1:1 and need adjustment; sugar is also used to tame tannin (e.g. in a New York Sour) and to cut bitterness/heat.

**Dilution** (§16.3, crediting Sasha Petraske of Milk & Honey): dilution has two dimensions — the water content at time of serving, and the *rate* of further dilution once on ice. Sasha's rule: better to serve slightly under-chilled with correct water content than perfectly cold but over-diluted. Full-glass ice service is partly about this — more ice = slower ongoing dilution after the drink reaches the table. M&H (not Match generally) uses hand-cracked, super-frozen slab ice for the same reason.

**Ice** (§17): Match uses **Hoshizaki** machines exclusively, producing large cubes at **28mm × 28mm × 32mm** — chosen because that size balances shaking, stirring, dilution rate and glass fit. Governing factors for ice quality: purity/water quality (clarity), cube size/surface-area-to-volume ratio (bigger cubes melt slower), and density/temperature (denser, colder ice = stronger bonds = slower melt; ice density ≈0.93 vs water's 1.0). House rules: fill glasses to the brim with ice unless asked otherwise; bartenders' hands never touch service ice (except bespoke hand-cracked prep); never reuse ice; any contamination (glass, blood, even a customer spitting in the well) means immediate full disposal.

## 5. Ingredients and house recipes for mixes

**Juice policy** (§19.1): lemon and lime are **always** squeezed fresh on-site daily and sieved; other juices (orange, grapefruit, cranberry, pineapple, apple) may be bought in from trusted suppliers (e.g. Eager) when fresh production isn't practical/cost-effective. Old and new juice batches are never combined.

**Standardized syrup/mix recipes** (§19.5–19.6) — the manual's explicit "you will be tested on these":

| Item | Ratio / recipe |
|---|---|
| **Sugar (simple) syrup** | 1:1 caster sugar : cold water (cold water only — warm water spoils faster and tastes worse) |
| **Honey syrup** | 2:1 honey : water (2 jars honey @340g + 340ml cold water per batch) |
| **Brown sugar syrup** | 1:1 brown sugar : cold water; unstable, lasts only 2–3 days before fermenting ("tastes like beer" — bad news for Caipirinhas) |
| **Tea syrup** | 1:1 brewed tea : sugar; brew fresh leaves (never bags) under 3 minutes to avoid tannin; only syrup made with hot water; keeps only a few days refrigerated |
| **Egg white mix** | 6–8 fresh egg whites + 50–75ml chilled water, lightly beaten to thin for pouring |
| **Match Spring Punch (MSP) mix** | 3.4L OJ/grapefruit carton base + 2 bottles Wyborowa vodka + 560ml crème de cassis + 560ml crème de framboise + 560ml sugar syrup |
| **Zombie mix (5 rums)** | 3.4L juice carton base + 1 bottle each: Goslings, Havana Blanco, Bacardi 8, Wood's 100, Doorly's 5yr |
| **Iced Tea mix** | 3.4L juice carton base + 1 bottle each: Wyborowa vodka, Havana Blanco, Tanqueray gin, Gran Centenario Plata tequila, Triple Sec |

A vitamin-C tablet can extend a syrup's shelf life by up to 5 days. Fresh-juice/puree shelf-life notes are itemized in detail: lemons/limes 7–14 days unrefrigerated for juicing, 10–21 days refrigerated for garnish use, garnish-cut fruit only ~24 hours; mint 4 days at 4–10°C, discolored leaves discarded; soft berries 2–4 days; stone fruit 2–3 days (few more chilled); pineapple/apples don't survive overnight once cut; olives/cherries/onions keep weeks to months in their brine.

## 6. Standards (§20) — daily operating procedure

- **Station set-up**: arrange the ice well/speed rail/shelving to minimize bartender movement; frequently-used items (napkins, mint for Mojitos) must be within arm's reach; station layout should evolve with sales trends (e.g. swap cognac for rum in the rail if cognac outsells it).
- **Opening checklist**: verify prior close-down, check refrigeration/electrical equipment, confirm deliveries, chill stock, taste-check all carried-over juices/purees (discard rather than "marry up" old with new), squeeze juices, cut garnish, fully stock stations, check float, brief staff on bookings/pre-orders.
- **Closing checklist**: marry up part-bottles, clear/store fruit, dispose of all ice (never held overnight), full station and peanut-rail deep clean (fruit-fly hazard), clean all bar tools, restock wine/beer/champagne for maximum next-day chill, cash up, log handover communication.
- **Par levels**: explained via a worked vodka-sales example (5-7-5-6-8-6-7-5-9-7 bottles/week) showing why par should sit above the historical average (e.g. 10–12, not 7) to absorb spikes without over-stocking.
- **Bill service**: totals hidden under a fold or business card; discretionary service charge applies to table service only; bar-service charge only added above £100; disputes go to the Head Bartender/manager.
- **Staff drink policy**: one free house beer or house wine per shift, post-shift only; further drinks at a set discount (e.g. 25%); drinking on shift is a dismissible offense (tasting a shaken cocktail excepted).
- **Cleaning rota**: daily items folded into open/close; weekly/monthly items (glass-washer salting, fridge deep-cleans, back-bar polishing) scheduled ahead of the busiest nights.

## 8. Beer, wine, champagne and glossary (§24–34)

Shorter category-education sections following a consistent template: definition → raw materials/manufacture → styles (Lager vs Ale; grape varieties; Champagne method) → history/origins → glossary → a "summary and crib sheet" for quick staff reference. These sections read as First-Tuesday-style teaching material rather than operational standards, covering topics such as "the Brewed Under Licence abomination" (a critique of UK lager licensing), the Parker effect on wine buying, and a "five things you need to remember about champagne" cheat sheet.

The **Manual Glossary** (§34) defines house jargon precisely, e.g.: *Eye line* (learned fill-level judgment using the Boston glass), *Cut* (how a pour is ended — "often directly proportionate to ego"), *Double frozen* (re-freezing ice to toughen its outer layer and slow melt), *Window* (the balance range a recipe should fall inside), *First Tuesday*, *Alumni* (the list of Match bartenders who've passed through the group, described as an honor "second only to... meeting God").
