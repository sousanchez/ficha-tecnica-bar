# Lowder's Classic Recipe Training Manual

**Source file:** `LowderClassicRecipeTrainingManual.pdf` (44 pages)
**Actual title on document:** "Lowder's Easy List of 178 (Modern) Classics to Know" — 2016, by Chris Lowder (Indie Bartender), foreword signed "@GetLowderNow"

**Note on naming:** despite the filename, this PDF's own title page is "Lowder's Easy List of 178 (Modern) Classics to Know" — the same title as the separate `LowdersEasyListof178ModernClassicsToKnow.xlsx` file in this project. This PDF is the narrative training-manual version (with prose notes per spirit and full recipes), while the xlsx (summarized separately in `lowders-178-modern-classics-list.md`) is a condensed spreadsheet list. They are companion documents from the same author/series, not duplicates — the PDF contains far more instructional text and only a subset of full recipes plus a name-only reference list.

## Structural overview

The manual is a training curriculum, not a full bar book. It has two major parts:

**Part 1 — Core recipes by spirit category (with teaching notes).** For each major spirit, Lowder gives a short essay on how that spirit behaves in cocktails, then exactly six "starter" recipes (three shaken/citrus, three stirred/boozy) — except for smaller categories (Tiki, Pisco, Sherry, Apple Brandy) which get fewer. Categories covered in order:
1. Gin
2. Vodka
3. Tequila/Mezcal
4. American Whiskey (Bourbon + Rye)
5. Irish Whiskey
6. Scotch
7. Rum (white + dark)
8. Tiki (7 recipes)
9. Cognac/Brandy
10. Pisco & Apple Brandy (combined)
11. Sherry (3 recipes)

**Part 2 — "102 More Drinks Everyone Should Know."** An alphabetized reference list (Core Call Drinks A-Z, then Highballs, Martinis, and Call Shots) giving recipe/build only, with no teaching prose — a fast-reference appendix for after the core 6-per-spirit list is mastered.

## Author's philosophy (from the Foreword)

- Written because junior/intermediate bartenders "share a common struggle": writing a bar recipe manual takes forever, managers don't have time, and there's too much disparate info to self-assemble.
- Explicitly **not** trying to be exhaustive or to list "the most delicious cocktails ever invented" — it's a curated *starter kit*.
- Core teaching principle: the **80/20 rule** — 80% of guest orders come from roughly 20% of the possible recipe universe, so mastery should start with a small, high-frequency core rather than memorizing an entire cocktail encyclopedia (explicitly likened to memorizing a Spanish dictionary vs. becoming fluent).
- **Why six drinks per spirit:** three shaken/citrus + three stirred/boozy per spirit is deliberately small and achievable, using ingredients with minimal disparate prep beyond basic juices/syrups.
- Bars/teams are explicitly invited to customize and add to the list — but only *after* mastering the base list, done together with the lead bartender so the whole team edits in sync ("Don't carve ice if you can't cook rice").
- No backstories/attribution are given for the classic recipes (author states he didn't invent any of them) — but he explicitly encourages bartenders to separately research the history of drinks that excite them, since "storytelling texture... can really bring a bar experience to life."
- The 102-drink appendix exists as a secondary goal list — not for immediate memorization, but to master over time or keep as quick reference behind the bar.

## Notable philosophy asides embedded in the spirit sections

- **On vodka & "fake Martini" guest requests (the manual's longest rant):** Bartenders should not sneer at requests for non-classic "Martinis" (Appletini, Chocolate Martini, etc.) by saying "we don't make those." The guest isn't citing a recipe — they're saying "I want to feel comfortable and have something I recognize, made well." Recommended response pattern: accept the request, then elevate it with quality ingredients (e.g., fresh-pressed apple juice instead of neon sour mix for an Apple Martini) and frame it as "I can absolutely make you one — mine uses fresh-pressed apple juice, so it won't be neon green, but it'll be the best Apple Martini you've had. Is that OK?" Vodka drinkers are called "the most fun guests" because they're often the least flavor-educated, giving bartenders the biggest opportunity to impress and win regulars.
- **On tequila/mezcal:** underrepresented in classic cocktails purely because agave spirits arrived in the US cocktail scene too late (after Prohibition) to enter the classic canon — not because they don't work; savory/grassy tequila needs savory/grassy partner flavors. Añejo tequila cocktails are rare mainly due to cost (analogous to rarely seeing an 18-year single malt cocktail).
- **On American whiskey:** bourbon (corn-based, sweeter/softer) generally works better shaken; rye (spicier, higher tannin) holds up better stirred against vermouths/liqueurs without getting lost.
- **On Irish whiskey:** "tragically underrepresented"; mild bottles (Jameson, Bushmills) can get lost shaken, but new premium releases (Redbreast, Connemara, Green Spot) open more cocktail possibilities — at higher cost, so price-awareness matters.
- **On Scotch:** high price is the real limiter, not flavor versatility. Standard technique: rinse the glass with an intense peated/Islay Scotch (e.g., Laphroaig) and build the actual cocktail with a cheaper blended Scotch — gives a balanced smoky accent without the cost of an all-Scotch pour. "A little bit goes a long, long way."
- **On rum:** an extremely broad flavor category; recipes deliberately avoid brand-specificity so bartenders can substitute based on availability, and even blend rum styles ("what one rum can't do, three can"). Stirred rum drinks are rare/tricky (rum's silky richness plus syrup can turn "flabby") but excellent when balanced.
- **On Tiki:** author's own working definition — a Tiki drink blends many syrups/juices/spirits (usually rums) into something rich, boozy, and fun. Crushed/cracked ice is used because these drinks are viscous and rich and benefit from extra dilution/chill; Tiki mugs are functional (ceramic insulates against ambient heat, keeping the drink tasting good longer), not just decorative.
- **On Cognac/Brandy:** priced for sipping, so bars typically stock only one grade (VS = younger/juicier, or VSOP = older/drier/tannic) — know your well cognac's profile since it changes how much sugar/citrus to balance with. Shaken cognac drinks were popular in the 1800s–early 1900s but became rarer as cognac got pricier; many old "cognac sours" from historic books aren't worth the good cognac — the Sidecar is called out as an enduring exception.
- **On Apple Brandy/Pisco:** grouped together for brevity, not because they're related. American apple brandy is drier/spicier (American oak); French apple brandy/calvados is juicier, with aged VSOP/Hors d'age bottlings closer to cognac. Important distinction flagged: **Applejack ≠ apple brandy** — applejack is a blend of apple brandy and American whiskey, a different product; the author suggests blending your own apple-brandy-plus-whiskey mix rather than buying commercial applejack if that specific flavor is wanted. For pisco, Peruvian is the author's preference (richer/silkier) over Chilean.
- **On Sherry:** author is enthusiastic ("Sherry is awesome") despite only including three recipes; sherry is salty/dry/savory/acidic — great as a seasoning ingredient in small doses but tricky as a cocktail base since it can turn a drink thin. Must know sherry classification (bone dry to syrupy sweet) before using it, and sherry/vermouth should always be refrigerated since lighter styles like Manzanilla oxidize within about a week.
- **On Falernum (Rum/Tiki section):** homemade is strongly preferred over the standard commercial John D. Taylor's — likened to homemade vs. store-bought mayonnaise ("once you start making it on your own, you will never turn back").

## Core recipes captured (representative, full ratios in source)

All recipes are given with build technique, glassware, and garnish. Selected house-standard techniques flagged in the text:

- **Manhattan** (2.5 oz bourbon or rye / 1 oz Italian Rosso vermouth / 3 dashes Angostura, stir/strain, brandied cherries) — **must** offer a choice of bourbon or rye, and a choice of coupe or rocks glass.
- **Old Fashioned** (2.5 oz bourbon or rye / <.5 oz Demerara syrup 1:1 / 3 dashes Angostura / .5 dash aromatic bitters, short stir, large rock, lemon+orange twists) — same "must offer whiskey choice" rule.
- **Margarita** (2 oz blanco tequila / .75 oz Cointreau [Combier preferred] / .75 oz fresh lime / barspoon simple syrup 1:1) — must offer a half-rim of salt only (never full rim) and a choice of coupe or rocks glass (rocks always gets a short straw); "Cadillac" variant swaps Grand Marnier for Cointreau.
- **Mai Tai** — must use house-made orgeat or a premium bottled one (Tiki Adam Kolesar or Small Hands Foods); never use bottled almond syrup ("wrong viscosity").
- **Penicillin** (2 oz blended Scotch / .75 oz lemon / .35 oz ginger syrup / .35 oz honey syrup 2:1, float .25 oz peated Scotch, NO STRAW) — appears twice (once in the Scotch section, once in the 102-drink appendix) with slightly different ratios, both floating peated Scotch on top with no straw so the aroma hits first.
- **Sazerac** — absinthe-rinsed *chilled* small rocks glass; twist expressed and discarded (no garnish left in glass).
- **East Side / "Old Maid"** — flagged as functionally the same build on different ice sizes, and noted to work with "any white spirit," not just gin.
- Full recipe details for Gin, Vodka, Tequila/Mezcal, Bourbon, Rye, Irish, Scotch, White/Dark Rum, Tiki (Beachbum, Fog Cutter, Jet Pilot, Painkiller, Scorpion, Three Dots & a Dash, 1934 Zombie), Cognac (Champs-Élysées, Coffee Cocktail, Georgia Julep, De La Louisiane, Japanese Cocktail, Vieux Carré), Pisco (Frantic Atlantic, Pisco Punch, Pisco Sour), Apple Brandy (American Trilogy, Grandfather, Widow's Kiss, Applejack Rabbit, Jack Rose, Philadelphia Fish House Punch), and Sherry (Adonis, Bamboo, Sherry Cobbler) are captured verbatim in the PDF with exact mL/oz measures, build order, glass, and garnish.

## The "102 More Drinks" appendix — categories

- **Core Call Drinks (A–Z):** ~65 named classics (Airmail, Amaretto Sour, Americano Highball, Aperol Spritz, Arsenic & Old Lace, Aviation, Bee's Knees, Bellini, Bijou, Black Russian, Bloody Mary, Boulevardier, Bramble, Brandy Alexander, Brooklynite, Caipirinha, Champagne Cocktail, Chartreuse Swizzle, Chrysanthemum, Clover Club, Corn 'n Oil, Daiquiri, East Side, French 75, Gimlet, Gin Rickey, Godfather/Godmother, Grasshopper, Hanky Panky, Hemingway Daiquiri, Hot Toddy, Irish Coffee, Japanese Cocktail, Kir, Kir Royale, Last Word, Long Island Iced Tea, Mai Tai, Manhattan, Margarita, Martinez, Mimosa, Mint Julep, Mojito, Moscow Mule/Mules, Negroni, Old Cuban, Old Fashioned, Old Maid, Paloma, Penicillin, Pimm's Cup, Piña Colada, Pineapple Daiquiri, Queen's Park Swizzle, Ramos Gin Fizz, Rob Roy, Rome With A View, Rusty Nail, Sazerac, Sidecar, Singapore Sling, Southside, Southside Fizz, Stinger, Ti' Punch, Tom Collins, Vieux Carré, White Russian, and more).
- **Bloody Mary** is flagged "à la minute if you really have to," with a make-ahead shelf-stable spice-mix recipe given (700 ml Worcestershire, 300 ml hot sauce, 120 ml ground black pepper, 100 ml smoked salt, dosed at >.5 oz per cocktail) for volume service; Caesar variant splits tomato juice with Clamato.
- **Highballs (12):** Bay Breeze, Cape Codder, Cuba Libre, Fuzzy Navel, Greyhound, "Highball" (Scotch+ginger ale), Madras, Presbyterian, Screwdriver, Sea Breeze, Seven & Seven, Shirley Temple — each with a one-line personality tag in quotes (e.g., Greyhound = "Very Graceful," Sea Breeze = "Very cool & gusty").
- **Martinis:** Gin family (Gibson, Gin Martini, Dry Gin Martini, Dirty Gin Martini), Vodka family (Vodka Martini, Dry Vodka Martini, Dirty Vodka Martini), and "Other" flavored martinis (Apple, Chocolate, Espresso, French, Grapefruit, Lychee) — note that classic Martinis get orange bitters *only* when garnished with a twist, never when garnished with an olive.
- **Call Shots** ("make drinks, not judgments"): B-52, Blow Job, Irish Car Bomb, Kamikaze, Lemon Drop, Melon Ball, Mind Eraser, Sex on the Beach — each with a one-line nickname. Closing tip: for a guest who wants "a really great shot," recommend breaking a lesser-known 3-ingredient sour (Gold Rush, Brooklynite, Bee's Knees) into shot glasses instead of straight liquor shots — one sour recipe serves about three people and is framed as both more responsible and a better flavor introduction than standard shots.

## Training-progression takeaways

1. Master 6 recipes per major spirit (36 total core drinks) before moving to the 102-drink reference list.
2. Do not customize/expand the list until the full core has been mastered as a team.
3. Learn the "why" behind each spirit's typical treatment (shaken vs. stirred tendencies, cost sensitivity, dilution needs) rather than memorizing recipes as arbitrary formulas.
4. Use guest-service judgment (especially on "fake" cocktail names and neat vs. flavored requests) as an extension of technical training, not a separate skill.
5. The 102-drink appendix is a "keep behind the bar for reference" tool, not a memorization requirement.
