## Chapter 7 — Traditional Cooking

### Grilling
- Charcoal was invented ~30,000 years ago; glowing coals reach **~1,100°C/2,000°F** (blackbody-orange). Propane/natural gas flames burn hotter (~1,900°C/3,500°F) but radiate far less heat because clean combustion emits little radiant energy.
- Gas grills compensate with lava rocks/ceramic plates/metal bars, which reach only **~800°C/1,500°F** — about 300°C/500°F below glowing charcoal.
- Measured heat flux: a gas grill at full tilt produces ~**5 W/cm² (110 BTU/h·in²)**; a charcoal grill can exceed **11 W/cm² (250 BTU/h·in²)** — more than double.
- BTU is a unit of energy, not power; manufacturers should quote BTU/h (or watts). What matters for cooking is **heat flux** = power ÷ grill area.
- Radiant heat travels in straight lines like light and casts "shadows"; raising food doesn't reduce peak intensity much until food is moved surprisingly far away. The **critical distance** at which food experiences full grill intensity equals **18.5% of grill width** for non-reflective sides, or **37%** for reflective sides. To cut heat in half on a 1.2 m/4 ft wide grill, food must rise to ~66 cm/26 in above the coals.
- Reflective (foil-lined) sides can extend the evenly-heated "sweet spot" to cover ~90% of the grill; black, non-reflective interiors (e.g., kettle grills) cannot achieve a large sweet spot even with foil, because reflection angles are wrong on a curved shape.

### Broiling
- Electric broiler elements (nichrome) can reach **2,200°C/4,000°F** but are typically capped near **1,200°C/2,200°F**. Catalytic gas broilers top out around **540°C/1,000°F** but convert **~80%** of gas's chemical energy to infrared light (vs. much less for ordinary gas broilers) and heat more evenly.
- Sweet-zone rule of thumb for an electric broiler: center of the even-heat zone sits ~**5 mm/0.2 in below the heating element**, plus **44%** of the distance between heating rods (e.g., rods 10 cm/4 in apart → sweet zone centered 4.9 cm/1⅞ in below the element).

### Roasting
- True roasting uses radiant heat at a deliberate distance, exploiting the inverse-square law (valid only when food is several feet from the fire — not applicable to grilling/broiling distances).
- Heat-penetration time scales with the square of food thickness — a 25 cm/10 in turkey takes **4×** as long to roast as a 12.5 cm/5 in hen.
- Spit-roasting (rotisserie) averages surface exposure so peak radiant heat stays high but average heat is lowered — unlike simply moving food away from the fire, which lowers both peak and average.

### Panfrying à la plancha / Sautéing / Stir-frying / Covered sautéing
- Sautéing: watery juices depress pan temperature to **≤100°C/212°F**, preventing browning — hence "plenty of free space" and a powerful burner are essential.
- Flipping frequently cooks food faster and more evenly than flipping once: with a 2.5 cm/1 in steak flipped every **15 s** vs. once, the sub-surface peak temperature was **~10°C/18°F lower**, the core reached target faster, and the cook could pull the steak **~4 min earlier** with ~**3 min** less resting.
- Pan physics: on a copper pan 25 cm/10 in wide × 2.5 mm/⅛ in thick over a 14 cm/5½ in gas burner, bottom temperature varies by no more than **22°C/40°F**; an equivalent stainless-steel pan would need to be **>70 mm/2¾ in thick** to perform as evenly (impractically heavy). Burner size — ideally as wide as the pan — matters more than pan material or thickness.
- Wok burners deliver up to **59,000 W (200,000 BTU/h)** vs. **4,400–8,800 W (15,000–30,000 BTU/h)** for Western professional gas burners and **1,750–4,100 W (6,000–14,000 BTU/h)** for domestic burners. Wok surfaces reach ~**1,200°C/2,200°F**, hot enough that woks warp and need reforging every few days and replacement every few weeks in busy kitchens. Two techniques: **bao** (true stir-fry, wok glowing dull red, food added in rapid succession) and **chao** (moderate heat, closer to covered sauté).

### Boiling / Steaming
- Boiling water conducts heat into food **2–3× faster** than near-boiling stagnant water because turbulence continuously replaces the boundary layer.
- At a full rolling boil, water at the bottom of the pot can superheat to **102–108°C/216–226°F**.
- Counterintuitively, **steaming is usually slower than boiling** for cooking most foods, because a "film condensate" layer forms on the food and insulates it, and trapped air pockets beneath that film add further insulation. (Steaming is still preferred for flavor/nutrient retention in vegetables — boiling leaches sugars, pigments, and vitamins.)
- Thick sauces can reach **110–130°C/230–266°F** at the pan bottom before "slug-and-column boiling" causes them to belch and scorch.

### Canning
- Napoleon offered a 12,000-franc prize for food preservation; Nicolas Appert's boil-and-seal method passed French navy tests in 1806, prize awarded 1809/1810. Englishman Peter Durand patented tin-can "canning" in 1810 (based on Appert's method). Clostridium botulinum wasn't identified as the danger requiring pressure canning until **1915** (USDA).
- **Boiling-water canning**: safe only for foods with pH **<4.6**; sterilize at **100°C/212°F for 5 minutes to >90 minutes** depending on food. At altitude: **10 min at 300–1,830 m/1,000–6,000 ft**, **15 min above that**.
- **Pressure canning**: required for low-acid (high-pH) foods (most vegetables, meats, seafood). Must reach **115°C/240°F or hotter** to eliminate C. botulinum spores (a "12D reduction" — one-in-a-trillion survival chance). At sea level, **1 bar/14.5 psi gauge pressure** ≈ **120°C/248°F**; total absolute pressure ≈ 2 bar/29 psi.
- Pressure gauges read **gauge pressure** (excess over atmospheric), not absolute — a common source of error; air trapped in the headspace (per Dalton's law of partial pressures) can make the gauge overstate the true water/steam temperature by many degrees, so **venting steam for ~10 minutes** before sealing is essential.
- Frigi-canning (E.F. Kohman, 1960) = today's cook-chill sous vide; helped make modern pasteurized milk distribution possible. Pasteur developed his namesake process in 1864–65 at the request of Napoleon III to fix wine spoilage.

### Pot-Roasting and Stewing (the "lost art" of authentic braising)
- Authentic pot-roasting = a sealed pot with a shallow layer of liquid, heated from all sides (lid covered in coals) — distinct from modern "braising," which is really stewing (submerging meat in liquid).
- Modern ovens are ventilated, so moisture escapes and volatile flavor compounds are lost; wet-bulb temperature in an open pot/pan can vary by **10°C/18°F** day to day with kitchen humidity, and total braising temperature can swing **~20°C/36°F** depending on pot material, color, fullness, and lid tightness.

### Baking
- The word "roast" dates to 1066 (Norman Conquest); "bake" traces back >5,000 years to drying mud bricks in ovens — most "roasted" oven foods (roast beef, roast turkey) are technically baked.
- **The real baking temperature is the wet-bulb temperature**, not the oven dial (dry-bulb) setting — it can never exceed 100°C/212°F and is largely set by humidity, which is controlled mostly by the food itself, not the cook.
- Preheating matters because oven walls (not air) store the heat reservoir; heating 0.14 m³/5 ft³ of air to 250°C/480°F takes only **42 kJ**, deliverable by a typical heating element in **~21 seconds** — nearly all the "preheating time" is actually about heating the walls.
- Three drying stages: settling period (surface rises to wet-bulb temp), constant-rate period (surface stays wet, evaporation dominates), falling-rate period (crust forms, boiling zone recedes inward).
- Convection ovens speed drying more than cooking per se; benefit is large for thin foods (bacon) and minimal for thick foods (roasts, hams).
- Oven thermostats can mismeasure dry-bulb temperature by as much as **14°C/25°F** at high baking temperatures.
- Radiant power scales with the 4th power of temperature: a wall section at 270°C/520°F radiates **16% more** intensely than one at 250°C/480°F — explaining why hot-spot variation matters far more at high bake temperatures.
- Counterintuitively, a fully loaded oven often bakes food **faster** than a lightly loaded one, because food-released humidity raises the real (wet-bulb) baking temperature.

### Cooking in Oil
- Deep-frying is essentially "baking, only faster": the same three drying stages occur, oil plays the dry-bulb role, water vapor bubbles from the food act as the wet-bulb/humidity mechanism.
- Typical preheat range for frying oil: **150–200°C/300–390°F**; frying above **200°C/390°F** risks burning the crust before the interior cooks.
- **Biot number** (ratio of surface heat-transfer rate to internal conduction rate) explains why small/thin foods cook evenly (chips) while large foods (chicken breasts) develop a doneness gradient; halving food volume can cut cooking time by up to a factor of **4**.
- Maillard/browning reactions proceed (more slowly) even at **160–165°C/320–330°F** without burning; multistage frying example: cook through at **120–140°C/250–285°F**, then finish at **180–200°C/355–390°F** for the crust.
- Frying French fries **10°C/18°F below** the recommended 180–185°C/355–365°F range can increase oil absorption by **40%**.
- **Common Frying Oils table** (melting/smoke/flash points): beef tallow melts 54°C/129°F, smokes 205°C/400°F, flash 260–290°C/500–554°F; duck fat and chicken fat (schmaltz) both melt 54°C/129°F, smoke 190°C/375°F; palm oil melts 37°C/99°F, smokes 215–230°C/420–445°F; pork lard melts 33°C/91°F, smokes 185–205°C/365–400°F; extra-virgin olive oil melts −6°C/21°F, smokes 210°C/410°F, flash 232°C/445°F; canola oil melts −17°C/1°F, smokes 225–245°C/435–475°F; grapeseed oil melts 10°C/50°F, smokes 200°C/390°F.
- Fresh oil browns food poorly because food in fresh oil is in direct contact with the oil for only about **one-tenth** of the cooking time (steam bubbles push oil away); "broken-in" oil (with emulsifiers formed via hydrolysis) raises contact time to over **half** the frying time, giving faster, more even browning — hence the tip of adding a few teaspoons of old oil to a fresh batch.
- Traditional confit is largely mystique: cooking-in-fat is "irrelevant to the final product," which depends mainly on time/temperature; oil penetrates only about **1 mm** into meat during cooling, and modern confit is easily replicated sous vide plus a post-cook coating of fat.

### Smoking
- Ideal smoldering temperature for flavor is **~400°C/750°F** (not the ignition point of wood, 233°C/451°F — wood can smolder without flame up to ~500°C/930°F).
- Lignin (20–33% of wood's dry weight) breaks down above **~300°C/570°F** into the phenols responsible for smoked flavor; it is otherwise indigestible.
- **Good Woods for Smoking table**: light (grapevine, straw, ash/elm/hornbeam/chestnut, tea leaves), medium (alder — excellent with seafood; apple; cherry/peach; corncob; heather; walnut; linden), bold (hickory, pecan — "pleasantly sweet"; beech — traditional with fish; juniper), very bold (camphor laurel — used for Chinese smoked duck; oak — "flavor overpowering for some foods"; mesquite — "smolders very hot... pungent").
- Only ~**10%** of smoke's volume is invisible vapor-phase compounds, but they do **>90%** of the flavoring/coloring/preserving work; visible smoke (soot + liquid droplets) contributes comparatively little because oil-like droplets bead on the wet food surface rather than penetrating.
- Smoke ring forms from nitrogen dioxide and carbon monoxide reacting with myoglobin — a watermark of penetration depth, not a flavor source itself, and only forms in muscle tissue.
- Ideal smoker relative humidity: **70–80%**. Hot-smoking fish above **60% RH at 50°C/122°F** (or lower RH at higher temps) gelatinizes collagen too fast and the flesh tears off the hook.
- Cold-smoking occurs at **20–30°C/68–85°F or lower** — too cool to pasteurize, hence the traditional reliance on curing salts (nitrate/nitrite) for safety during multi-day/week smoking.

## Chapter 8 — Cooking in Modern Ovens

### CVap / water-vapor ovens
- Invented by Winston Shelton (also inventor of the KFC pressure fryer) to independently control **Doneness** (wet-bulb temperature, via a heated water pan) and **Browning** (dry-bulb temperature, via side heating elements, scale 0–10).
- Doneness/wet-bulb temperature typically stays within **1–5°C/2–9°F** of set point once stabilized — takes **~45 minutes** to reach target from room temperature.
- At maximum Browning setting (10), dry-bulb temperature tops out at only **180°C/356°F** — a relatively modest ceiling for true browning, reflecting CVap's holding-cabinet heritage.
- Recommended "hotter-than-core" wet-bulb targets: **45°C/113°F** for fish, **50–55°C/122–131°F** for rare-to-medium-rare meat, **60–70°C/140–158°F** for braised dishes.

### Combi ovens
- Rational was the first combi oven brand and remains a market leader; ~**$12,000** (2010) for a half-sheet-pan programmable unit, as little as **$2,000** for compact commercial models.
- "Rational humidity" is not relative humidity — it's the mass fraction of water vapor in the air/water mixture, and most of its 0–100% nominal scale is physically unreachable below the boiling point. Practically, only **0%** (dehydrate) and **100%** (low-temp steam, wet-bulb = dry-bulb) are meaningfully distinct settings below 100°C/212°F.
- Measured wet-bulb behavior: convection mode at 0% Rational humidity gives wet-bulb ≈ **45°C/113°F** (range 42–48°C/108–118°F) regardless of dry-bulb setting below boiling; above the boiling point at 0% Rational humidity, wet-bulb ≈ **84°C/183°F** (80–88°C/176–190°F); combi mode ≥30% Rational humidity above boiling gives wet-bulb ≈ **84°C/183°F** (80–88°C/176–190°F) — more reliable than convection because moisture is actively added.
- Combi ovens fluctuate **~10°C/18°F** over 10–20 minutes even when well calibrated; fans reverse every 4 minutes, causing momentary local blips.
- Combi ovens come to temperature in **5–10 minutes** and steam large batches (e.g., 4.5 kg/10 lb broccoli) far faster than a water bath or CVap, but perform poorly below **60°C/140°F** — a dedicated water bath is recommended for fish/red meat sous vide-style cooking at low temperature.
- Dehydration in convection mode: 0% Rational humidity at **30–90°C/85–195°F**.

### Microwaves
- Boiling browns nothing because heat penetrates via conduction after the last surface water evaporates, and there's too little residual water left at that moment for microwaves to act on; browning also requires heating a layer **10–20× thicker** than the <1 mm/1/32 in layer a broiler browns.
- Microwaves are electromagnetic radiation, **300 MHz–300 GHz** — same family as visible light, unrelated to nuclear radiation.
- Myth-busting: microwaves penetrate only a couple of centimeters (not "inside-out" cooking — the rest heats by conduction); metal itself is fine in ovens, but sharp points/edges cause arcing.
- TurboChef hybrid ovens add convection heat up to **260°C/500°F** to solve the browning problem.

## Chapter 9 — Cooking Sous Vide

### History and rationale
- Origin story: a September 1965-era dinner (chef Joël Robuchon's kitchen, Michel Cliché) served pre-cooked, reheated food aboard a French bullet train without quality loss — an early proof of concept for what became "sous vide."
- Comparison photo: pan-roasted venison tenderloin to 52°C/126°F core showed **>40%** of the meat overcooked/gray; the sous vide version at the same core temperature was evenly done edge-to-edge.
- A computer-controlled water bath with good stirring fluctuates **less than half a degree** above/below set point.
- Beef short ribs braised sous vide at **58°C/136°F for 72 hours**; eggs poached at **65°C/149°F** give a custard-like texture.

### Packaging & food safety myths
- Vacuum-sealed food does **not** experience a true vacuum inside the bag — atmospheric pressure (≈1,013 mbar/14.7 psi at sea level) still presses on the bag and its contents; the exception is foods with internal air pockets (e.g., raspberries), which can collapse.
- Even a 75% vacuum during sealing leaves more than enough oxygen to defeat the preservative purpose of vacuum packing.
- Safe plastics for sous vide: high-density polyethylene, low-density polyethylene, polypropylene (same as Saran Wrap and name-brand storage bags). PVC/PVDC catering wraps and cracked/crazed polycarbonate (BPA) containers are the real concerns.
- Myth debunked: pre-blanching vacuum-sealed food above 80°C/176°F is *not* required for safety, though it is a legitimate extra safety margin in some cases.
- Pasteurized sous vide food storage limits: **≤3 days at 5°C/41°F**, **≤30 days at 1°C/34°F**, **unlimited below −20°C/−4°F**.

### Equipment and temperature control
- **Bang-bang controllers** (simple on/off thermostats) cause the bath to oscillate above/below set point; **PID controllers** modulate heating power proportionally (and track the rate of change, the "derivative" term) to converge smoothly, typically holding within **<0.5°C/1°F**.
- Standard 120V/15A North American circulators: max ~1,000–1,800 W, sufficient for **20–30 L/21–32 qt** baths under good conditions (covered, insulated); 240V/10A European circuits allow up to 2,400 W.
- Equipment comparison table: noncirculating bath (cheap, hot/cold spots), circulating water bath (accurate, usually ≤30 L/32 qt on standard household power), water-vapor oven (large capacity, slower for small portions, capped temperature), combi oven (large capacity, high temp, less accurate below 60°C/140°F, complex install), pressure cooker/canner (cheap, manual control, can exceed 100°C/212°F), autoclave (very expensive, fully automatic, up to **140°C/284°F**), pot on stove (cheapest, hardest to control), PID-retrofitted appliance (~$100 total, uneven flow unless bubbled with an aquarium pump).
- Liquid nitrogen freezing note (cross-referenced from Ch.9 chilling section into Ch.10): ultralow freezers can reach **−80°C/−112°F**.

### Cooking strategies
- **Equilibrium cooking** (bath set 1–2°C above target core temp): food cannot overcook, but cooking is slow. Example: a 30 mm/1¼ in steak in a 56°C/133°F bath reaches target in **~87 min** (close enough by 77 min).
- **Hotter bath** (e.g., 85°C/185°F): same steak reaches target core in only **~23¾ min** but must rest ~10 min more as core drifts upward; risk of overshoot is high — **40%** of the steak would exceed 60°C/140°F (medium rather than medium-rare) in this example.
- **Two-bath strategy**: sear/blanch, then a 45–50°C/113–122°F tenderizing bath (activating enzymes) for **4 h**, then a hotter finishing bath (e.g., to 56°C/133°F core) for an additional **~55 min**; total tenderizing benefit continues over **24–48 h**.
- **Cold-shocking myth**: plunging cooked food into ice water does **not** meaningfully "stop the cooking" — peak core temperature differs by less than **0.5°C/1°F** (thick steak) or **~1°C/2°F** (thin steak) compared to a tepid-water rest, because heat is already in transit toward the core when the food is pulled.
- Cooking-from-frozen must use the "bath at final core temperature" strategy (not hotter-than-core, since thaw time is unpredictable) and must be done at **≥54°C/129°F** to prevent bacterial growth during the extended thaw.
- Blanching bacterial kill rates: water at **70°C/158°F** decontaminates a surface in **~10 seconds**; at **76°C/168°F**, in **~1 second**. Enzyme destruction (polyphenol oxidase): 90% destroyed in **2 seconds at 100°C/212°F** vs. **~2 minutes at 90°C/194°F**.
- Presearing triggers Maillard reactions starting around **130°C/266°F**; once initiated, reactions continue even as temperature later drops.

## Chapter 10 — The Modernist Kitchen

### Extracting flavors (stocks, jus)
- Classic Escoffier veal stock mirepoix ratio: onion:carrot:celery = **2:1:1** by weight; bones/meat portion ≈ **5×** the mirepoix volume; meat cut into **2.5 cm/1 in** cubes.
- Fick's first law of diffusion explains why finely diced/ground ingredients extract flavor far faster: halving particle thickness extracts flavor **4× faster** (extraction time scales with the square of the smallest dimension). Grinding meat/veg can cut simmer time from ~16 hours to ~2 hours (factor of 8+) and reduce the amount of meat needed (e.g., 10 kg/22 lb coarse veal → 2.5 kg/5.5 lb ground veal for equivalent intensity).
- Pressure-cooking stock: at **1 bar/15 psi gauge pressure**, water boils up to **120°C/248°F**; pressure prevents boiling (which would emulsify fat/particles and cloud the stock). Dave Arnold/Nils Noren experiments found professional-chef panels consistently preferred **unvented** pressure-cooked stock over vented.
- Best Bets for Stock Aromatics table gives vegetable/herb/spice ratios by stock type (e.g., vegetable stock: onion 33%, carrot 25%, leek 8%, tomato 8%, celery 5%, mushroom 5%; standard herb/spice scalings 0.01–1% range).
- Jus vs. stock: no more than about **20%** of the water inside a roast migrates to the surface to feed Maillard-generating reactions, which is why jus yield is always small but intensely flavored.
- "Best Bets for Jus" and "Thickening Strategies for Sauces, Jus, and Glazes" tables cover reduce-on-stovetop (fast, low yield, strips volatiles), reduce-by-vacuum (moderate, more vibrant flavor), concentrate-with-agar, concentrate-by-freezing (best for many liquids), thicken-with-fat/fluid-gel/gum/pregelatinized-starch.
- Seasoning tables: salt 0.5–2.0% (most people prefer 0.75–1% for savory foods); MSG 0.1–2.0%; 5' ribonucleotides 0.02%; bacon 3–15%; fish sauce 2–10%; miso 1–8%; soy sauce 2–5%. Acid tables: cider/red-wine/malt vinegars pH ~2.4; lemon juice pH 2.3; lime juice pH 1.8–2; citric acid (diluted 1:10) pH 2.4; tartaric acid pH 2.4.

### Infusing essences
- Extraction methods: cold-pressing citrus peel oils; steam distillation (separates into an aromatic hydrosol, e.g., rose water, plus a richer essential-oil layer); solvent extraction with nonpolar solvents (hexane, ether) yielding "concretes"/"oleoresins," further processed into "absolutes" via alcohol washing (a "tincture"); supercritical CO₂ extraction (also used industrially to decaffeinate coffee); enfleurage (fat absorbs aroma from botanical material over several days, an ancient perfumery technique).
- Essential oils are deceptively different-smelling when concentrated (e.g., black pepper oil "doesn't even smell particularly spicy"); always predilute before use.

### Juicing
- Freezing-and-thawing fruit is a legitimate juicing method: ice crystals rupture cell walls, concentrating flavor compounds in the liquid that emerges first upon thawing — good for berries.
- Pectinase enzyme maceration (vacuum-sealed, refrigerated hours to days, or warmed <50°C/122°F for up to 4 h) improves juice yield from tough produce (apples, pears, carrots) while avoiding browning/bitterness from mechanical juicing.
- Osmotic juicing: coat fruit/veg in sugar or salt, warm up to **60°C/140°F** to accelerate.
- Browning is driven by polyphenol oxidase (PPO) released when cells rupture; countermeasures: keep juice cold, strain pulp promptly (PPO concentrates there), add ascorbic acid or sulfites/sodium benzoate.
- Example recipes use **centrifuging at 27,500g for 1 hour** repeatedly to clarify juices (Meyer Lemonade, Melon Water, Pistachio Consommé, Oxtail Consommé) — this exact spec (27,500g / 1 h) recurs throughout the book as the house standard for clarification.

### Filtering
- Freeze-filtering (a gel — often agar — clarifies a liquid via slow melt/thaw and syneresis) was popularized in Modernist circles via discussions between a chef named Klock, Heston Blumenthal, and Albert Adrià at an INICON conference; Dave Arnold and Nils Noren (French Culinary Institute) refined it for speed/yield.
- Example: Oxtail Consommé uses methylcellulose (Dow "Super Methylcellulose SGA 150") whisked into ground beef to form a raft/paste that clarifies the consommé by simmering ~45 min.

### Concentrate!
- **Strategies for Concentrating table**: stove-top reduction (cheap, easy, loses volatiles); vacuum reduction (rotavap-style, cheap setup, volatiles still escape); vacuum concentration (captures volatiles, expensive/complex, precise temp/pressure, handles large batches); freeze concentrating (simple, handles up to 6 liquids at once with precise temp control and low-speed centrifugation — describing the Genevac Rocket); reverse osmosis (energy-efficient, used in winemaking, not yet adapted to culinary use in 2011).
- Simple vacuum-reduction rig: heavy-walled Erlenmeyer flask + faucet or motorized aspirator; lowering pressure lowers water's boiling point, allowing "reduction" near or below room temperature.
- **Rotary evaporator ("rotavap")**: invented by Lyman Craig, described in *Analytical Chemistry* in 1950, first sold commercially by Büchi in 1957. Typical vacuum pump can reduce pressure by a factor of ~100, from 1,013 mbar/14.7 psi down to as low as **10 mbar/0.15 psi**. Rule of thumb: set bath temperature **20°C/36°F above** the target boiling point and condenser **20°C/36°F below** it. Worked example: to distill ethanol at 40°C/104°F, set vacuum to 167 mbar/125 torr, bath to 60°C/140°F, condenser to ≤20°C/68°F.
- Boiling-point-vs-vacuum table: at 20 mbar/17 torr water boils at 20°C/68°F; at 194 mbar/145 torr water boils at 60°C/140°F (ethanol boils far lower at every pressure).
- **Genevac Rocket Evaporator**: combines vacuum distillation, steam-based heating, and low-speed centrifugation; at its lowest pressure (**6 mbar/4.5 torr**, 2010 model) water boils even below its normal freezing point; processes up to **6 bottles** simultaneously; costs "tens of thousands of dollars" (2011). Example recipe (Mock Turtle Soup, Heston Blumenthal) reduces stock at **8 mbar/6 torr, 50°C/122°F bath, 1°C/34°F condenser**.
- **Freeze concentration**: exploits freezing-point depression — used historically for applejack ("jacking"), ice wine (eiswein — natural version requires a hard freeze during harvest; cryoextraction is the artificial equivalent), and ice beer. Cannot achieve high concentration ratios or full purity because some solutes get trapped in the ice.
- **Reverse osmosis**: forces liquid through a semipermeable membrane at **2–69 bar/30–1,000 psi**; used by vintners to concentrate grape juice by discarding the permeate (pure water).

### Drying
- Water activity (aw) scale: pure water = 1.0; juices/milk ≈ 0.97; raisins ≈ 0.76; crackers/dry cookies ≈ 0.3; powdered milk/instant coffee ≈ 0.2; dried beans ≈ 0.25. Most bacteria stop growing at aw **≤0.91**; yeasts below **~0.87**.
- Dehydrator cabinets operate **40–70°C/105–160°F**. Food safety floor for drying: food itself (not chamber air) must stay above **52°C/126°F** to suppress bacterial growth; slicing to **<1 cm/⅜ in** and drying above **40°C/105°F** is generally enough to minimize bacterial proliferation (though not to pasteurize).
- **Spray-drying**: liquid at 40–60% solids by mass is atomized into **180–250 micron** droplets in a heated tower; lab-scale (~1 m/3 ft column) blows air at **90–300°C/195–570°F**, cools to ~100°C/212°F on contact with the mist, and evaporative cooling drops the droplets themselves to only ~**40°C/105°F** — minimal heat damage. Example recipes: Spray-Dried Buttermilk (inlet 90°C/194°F, outlet ~57°C/135°F, ~2 h) and Spray-Dried Blood Orange Juice (inlet 100°C/212°F, outlet ~65°C/149°F, ~2 h).
- **Freeze-drying**: works via sublimation under high vacuum (**<6 mbar/4.5 torr**), ice going directly to vapor without melting, preserving delicate microstructure far better than conventional drying. Critical ("collapse") temperatures: meats **≈−12 to −10°C/−14 to −10°F**; seafood **−12 to −5°C/10–23°F**; plant foods often as low as **−50 to −35°C/−58 to −31°F**. Slice thickness scales drying time roughly with the square of thickness: **6 mm/¼ in slices ≈ 4 hours**; **1.2 cm/½ in ≈ 16 hours**; a whole steak can take **several days**. A secondary higher-temperature stage removes the remaining bound ("vicinal") water. Fat does not freeze-dry, so fatty cuts should be pre-frozen fast and stored cold/dark afterward to limit rancidity.

### Cryogenic freezing and carbonating
- Liquid nitrogen: boiling point **−196°C/−321°F**, freezing point **−210°C/−346°F**; "cryogenics" is technically defined as producing temperatures below **−150°C/−238°F**. Temperature differential with room-temperature food can exceed **200°C/360°F**, driving very fast heat transfer — though, surprisingly, LN2 actually absorbs *less* total energy per unit mass than ice does, making it a poor bulk chiller despite being excellent for flash effects.
- Cryosearing (frozen meat straight into a hot fryer) exploits a **~400°C/750°F** gap between the frozen surface and the frying oil, allowing crisp skin to form before the interior overcooks.
- Dry ice sublimes directly to gas at **−78°C/−173°F**, expanding by a factor of **>500** in volume — only ~**3 g/0.1 oz** of dry ice carbonates 355 mL/12 oz of water into seltzer. Humans exhale ~1 kg/2.2 lb of CO₂ per day.
- Safety: normal air is 21% oxygen; a nitrogen gas influx can dilute a poorly ventilated room to **below 16%** oxygen, the threshold for loss of consciousness (citing the 1986 Lake Nyos, Cameroon CO₂ disaster that killed ~1,700 people and 3,500 livestock as a real-world cautionary parallel). Liquid nitrogen beads harmlessly off skin via the Leidenfrost effect (unlike hot frying oil) but must never be sealed in a rigid container (explosion risk from rapid gas expansion).
- 2009 *Science* paper (cited in the text) traced the sour taste of carbonation to sour-taste-bud cells: carbonic anhydrase 4 on the tongue converts CO₂ into bicarbonate + hydrogen ions, and the hydrogen ions trigger sour receptors — meaning fizziness is partly a taste, not just a tactile, sensation.
- Truffle storage in CO₂ (three methods shown: siphon-charged bag, ~3 g/0.1 oz dry ice pellet in a sealed container, or a gas-flushing chamber vacuum sealer) significantly extends freshness; the same trick works for leafy greens.
