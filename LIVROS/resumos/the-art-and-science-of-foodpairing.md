# The Art & Science of Foodpairing: 10,000 Flavour Matches That Will Transform the Way You Eat

**Authors:** Peter Coucquyt, Bernard Lahousse, Johan Langenbick
**Publisher:** Mitchell Beazley
**Source file:** `726429500-The-Art-Science-of-Foodpairing-...-Z-Library.pdf` (388 pages)

The authors are the founders of **Foodpairing** (foodpairing.com), the Belgium-based company/database launched in 2009 that pioneered "aroma-matching" as a professional culinary R&D tool, used by Michelin-starred chefs, bartenders, and food brands worldwide.

## 1. Structural Overview

Per the book's own Contents page:

1. **Introduction (pp. 7–36)**
   - *The Story of Foodpairing* (pp. 8–12) — origin narrative, told partly in first person by Bernard Lahousse, plus sidebar essays by **Heston Blumenthal** (The Fat Duck) and the François Benzi/Heston Blumenthal "caviar and white chocolate" discovery story.
   - *Key Aromas* (pp. 13–17) — volatile organic compounds (VOCs), gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS), key odorants/odour thresholds, synthetic (gestalt) aroma perception, and interactions between aroma molecules (synergy, inhibition, suppression, overshadowing).
   - *Olfaction versus Gustation* (pp. 18–21) — orthonasal vs. retronasal smell, taste-bud biology, trigeminal sensation, supertasters.
   - *The Importance of Aroma* (pp. 22–24) — evolutionary/biological role of smell, flavour-flavour learning (Pavlov, evaluative conditioning).
   - *Aroma Molecules* (pp. 25–27) — precursors (carbohydrates, amino acids, fatty acids), Maillard reaction, caramelization, fermentation, and a taxonomy of aroma "building blocks": Aldehydes, Alcohols, Ketones, Esters, Lactones, Acids, Terpenes, Furans/furanoids, Furanones, Phenols.
   - *Building Your Aroma Library* (pp. 28–29) — how to train your palate/nose.
   - *How Foodpairing Works* (pp. 30–36) — the proprietary **14 Aroma Types / 70 Aroma Descriptors** system, how to read an **Aroma Wheel** and a **Pairing Grid**, and "From Pairing Ingredients to Delicious Dishes" (balancing texture and taste on top of aroma).
2. **Ingredients & Pairings (pp. 39–371)** — "From Kiwi to Oyster": ~90 ingredient chapters, each with an aroma wheel, ingredient essay, at least one professional chef's recipe/pairing sidebar, and multiple **pairing grids** (each listing ~10 ingredient pairings with dot-matrix charts across the 14 aroma types).
3. **Index of Ingredients (p. 372)**, Glossary of UK & US Terms, Endnotes, Acknowledgements, About Foodpairing (p. 387–388).

## 2. Core Methodological Framework

- **Founding hypothesis** (same lineage as Blumenthal/Benzi): *ingredients that share key aroma molecules at the right concentration pair well.* The company's own origin story is the **kiwître** — chef Sang-Hoon Degeimbre's dish pairing raw oyster with kiwi, discovered after Bernard Lahousse and bioengineer Jeroen Lammertyn ran GC–MS analysis and found kiwi shares green/grassy/fatty marine-like aldehydes with oysters.
- **Two independent discoveries are credited as the field's origin points**: François Benzi (Firmenich) noticing jasmine and liver both contain indole (1992), and Heston Blumenthal's caviar + white chocolate pairing (also explained post-hoc via shared aroma compounds by Benzi).
- **VOCs and GC–MS**: >10,000 volatile organic compounds identified in food to date; a GC–MS separates a food sample's compounds by molecular weight/retention time and quantifies each by peak area. Aroma compounds are often present at trace levels (10–15 mg/kg).
- **Key odorants**: only a fraction of the VOCs detectable by GC–MS actually exceed the human "odour recognition threshold" and thus contribute to perceived flavour (e.g., coffee has 1,000+ detectable VOCs but only ~30–40 are perceptible key odorants).
- **Aroma is synthetic, not additive**: mixtures of more than ~4 odorants stop being perceived as their individual components and instead create a new, emergent "gestalt" smell (e.g., no single molecule smells like "strawberry" — it's a synthesis of fruity esters + coconut-like lactones + caramellic furanones + green/cheesy notes). Foodpairing uses machine-learning models to predict this synthetic perception.
- **Molecule interactions**: synergy/additivity, inhibition, suppression, and overshadowing/hypoadditivity — four documented ways aroma molecules combine non-linearly in mixtures (illustrated with dose-response graphs).
- **Aroma building blocks** (chemical functional groups covered): Aldehydes (hexanal, nonanal, undecanal, vanillin, cinnamaldehyde, benzaldehyde), Alcohols (geosmin, 1-octen-3-ol), Ketones (beta-damascenone, beta-ionone, filbertone), Esters (ethyl butanoate, ethyl hexanoate, isopentyl acetate), Lactones (gamma/delta lactones, whisky lactone, jasmine lactone), Acids (acetic acid), Terpenes/terpenoids/sesquiterpenes (limonene, pinene, menthol, linalool, geranial/neral), Furans/furanoids (sotolon), Furanones (furaneol), Phenols (eugenol).
- **The 14 Aroma Types & 70 Descriptors system** (Foodpairing's proprietary taxonomy, used to build every wheel/grid in the book): Fruity (apple, banana, berry, coconut, fruity, grape, peach, pineapple, tropical) · Citrus (citrus, grapefruit, lemon, orange) · Floral (floral, geranium, honey, rose, violet) · Green (cucumber, fatty, grass, green, oat flakes, waxy) · Herbal (herbal, mint, thyme) · Vegetable (bell pepper, cabbage, celery, garlic, mushroom, onion, potato) · Caramel (caramel, maple) · Roasted (coffee, earthy, fried, malty, popcorn, roasted) · Nutty (hazelnut, nutty, tonka) · Woody (balsamic, phenolic, pine, smoky, woody) · Spicy (anise, camphor, cinnamon, clove, cumin, pungent, spicy, vanilla) · Cheesy (acidic, buttery, cheesy, creamy) · Animal (animal, fishy, meaty) · Chemical (burnt, dusty, petroleum, soapy, solvent).
- **Aroma Wheels**: a two-ring radial chart per ingredient — inner ring = the 14 aroma types present/absent (greyed out if absent); outer ring = concentration bands for each of the 70 descriptors, sized by distance from center and by band thickness/length.
- **Pairing Grids**: a table with the main ingredient in bold at top and up to 10 suggested pairing ingredients below, each row rendered as 14 coloured dots (one per aroma type) — dot size indicates a *shared, specific aroma molecule* between the two ingredients, not just "same category."
- **Classic vs. Potential vs. Chef's pairings**: like Book 1's Best/Surprising split, this book labels each featured pairing as a "Classic pairing" (culturally established), "Potential pairing" (data-suggested, novel), or attributes it directly to a named professional chef (e.g., Dan Barber of Blue Hill at Stone Barns for celeriac + Cheddar + miso).
- **From aroma-pairing to a finished dish**: a dedicated section (p. 35–36) on layering **texture contrast** (soft vs. crunchy/crispy — 60 texture types identified), **taste contrast** (sweet/salty/sour/bitter/umami, with a diagram of which tastes enhance, reduce, or balance one another), and **"aromatic complexity"** management — via "overtoning" (closely related aromas layered for subtlety) vs. deliberately contrasting aroma profiles, referencing psychologist Daniel Berlyne's "unity-in-variety" aesthetic principle, and using the Mexican dish *mole de pollo* as the paradigm case of successfully balanced complexity.

## 3. Sample Ingredient Pairing Data (organized)

### Kiwi (p. 40–43)
- **Signature dish:** *Kiwître* (Sang-Hoon Degeimbre, L'Air du Temps, Belgium) — raw oyster over diced kiwi, croutons, lime-coconut cream.
- **Classic pairing:** kiwi + melon (shared tropical ester ethyl 2-methylpropanoate).
- **Potential pairings:** kiwi + chayote squash (boiled); kiwi berry + quail; kiwi + apple (shared aldehyde hexanal, also found in olive oil).
- Kiwi berry pairing-grid sample (top rows): pan-fried quail, hazelnut, purple sage, baked king scallop, aji mirasol chilli, dry-aged beef, boiled pumpkin, black garlic purée, usukuchi (light soy sauce), basil.
- Grid ingredients for "melon": grilled green asparagus, granadilla, roast pork liver, turnip-rooted chervil, boiled brown crab meat, aji panca chilli, Gruyère, guava, Korean fish sauce, kiwi.

### Apple (p. 44–49)
- Traced to a single wild ancestor, *Malus sieversii* (Kazakhstan/Xinjiang), cross-bred with the tart European crab apple *Malus sylvestris*.
- Character-impact compounds shared across all apple varieties: hexyl acetate (generic apple), hexanal/trans-2-hexenal/butanal (green apple, fruity complexity).
- **Cultivar-specific aroma wheels compared:** Cox's Orange Pippin (dominant: beta-damascenone → rose/floral), Elstar (tart, citrusy, clove-spiced), Jonagold (coconut- and banana-scented, more tropical), Boskoop (fruity/tropical, geranium, anise, oat flakes).
- **Classic pairings:** apple + cheese (Jonagold + Brie, Braeburn + Parmesan); apple sauce + potato (Dutch *hete bliksem*/"heaven and earth" mash); apple + caramel; Braeburn apple + rolled oats (Bircher muesli); apple + celeriac rémoulade.
- **Potential pairings:** apple + hay; Elstar apple + cooked quinoa; apple sauce + prickly pear cactus.
- Grid sample for "cooked quinoa": boiled parsnip, European bay leaf, pickled sakura leaf, corn smut, tomato ketchup, miyabi melon, red bell pepper, mature Cheddar, sencha tea, white truffle.

### Celeriac (p. 50–53)
- Shares ~70% of its volatile compounds with celery stalks; raw celeriac is citrusy/woody/pine-like, cooked celeriac becomes sweeter and creamier with mint/honey notes.
- **Chef's pairing:** Dan Barber (Blue Hill at Stone Barns, NY) — whole-plant celeriac dish using root, leaves, and secondary roots; braised celeriac with Cheddar-miso foam, celeriac-leaf sauce, celeriac-skin tea.
- **Classic dish:** apple and celeriac rémoulade (French).
- **Potential pairings:** raw celeriac + brown shrimp; celeriac + lavender; celeriac + vanilla (shared vanillin/floral-rose notes; also barley malt).
- Grid sample for "boiled celeriac": orange, aji amarillo chilli, avocado, pan-fried guinea fowl, goats' cheese, roasted peanut, baked dab, Alphonso mango, cow's milk yogurt, roasted sweet potato.

### Vanilla (p. 54–57)
- *Vanilla planifolia* (Madagascar Bourbon vanilla, 75% of world production) vs. *Vanilla tahitensis* (Tahitian vanilla, more anise/floral, less classic "vanilla" than Bourbon).
- Vanillin makes up ~82% of Bourbon vanilla's aroma profile; also phenolic/balsamic, cheesy, fruity-coconut and peach lactones.
- **Classic pairings:** vanilla + cream (vanillin is fat-soluble, intensifies in high-fat matrices); vanilla + chocolate (Aztec *xocoatl*).
- **Potential pairings:** white asparagus + vanilla (beurre blanc); vanilla + shiro (white soy sauce).
- Grid sample for "Bourbon vanilla": oven-baked pork loin rib steak, roasted red bell pepper, roast lamb, cooked brown rice, turnip-rooted chervil, boiled brown shrimp, boiled potato, tomato, boiled brown crab meat, Camembert.

### Chocolate (p. 58–60)
- History: Olmec/Aztec *xocoatl*; three cacao varieties (Criollo, Forastero, Trinitario); fermentation → drying/roasting → conching stages, each generating new aroma compounds (Maillard products in roasting; caramel/spicy/cheesy compounds in conching).
- **Dark chocolate aroma wheel**: dominant types are Roasted, Nutty, Caramel, Cheesy/Acidic, Vanilla, Woody, plus a distinct Vegetal/green thread (bell pepper, cabbage, garlic, mushroom, onion, potato) enabling savoury-vegetable pairings.
- **Classic pairing:** milk chocolate + strawberry (shared molecule: strawberry furanone/4-hydroxy-2,5-dimethyl-3(2H)-furanone).
- **Potential pairing:** dark chocolate + broccoli (and other vegetables — beetroot, butternut squash, courgette, cucumber, parsnips, peas, potatoes, tomatoes).
- Grid sample for "dark chocolate": cassia bark (Chinese cinnamon), white button mushroom, rhubarb, lamb's lettuce (mâche), pickled sakura leaf, boiled broccoli, acacia honey, Tahitian vanilla, shiso leaf, peanut butter.
- Milk and white chocolate get their own comparative mini aroma-wheels and pairing grids (milk chocolate: sobrassada, Bourbon vanilla, fish miso, sesame halva, boiled squid, Mara des Bois strawberry, Hennessy VS cognac, boiled lobster, toasted sesame seeds, grilled green asparagus; white chocolate: lemon balm, elderberry, apple juice, pisco, açaí berry, smoked bacon, horseradish purée, peppermint, ground black pepper, red grapes).

## Note on relation to Book 3

`foodpairing-arte-e-ciencia-pt.md` documents the Portuguese-language edition of this exact book — same three authors (Peter Coucquyt, Bernard Lahousse, Johan Langenbick), same "10,000 flavour matches," same 14-aroma-type / 70-descriptor system, aroma wheels, and pairing grids, translated/localized for the Brazilian/Portuguese market. It is not an independent title but the official translated edition of *The Art & Science of Foodpairing*.
