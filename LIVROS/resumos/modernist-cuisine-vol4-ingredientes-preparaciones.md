## Chapter 13 — Thickeners

### Core concepts
- Thickening works by three basic mechanisms: (1) suspending solid/bulking particles in a liquid to form a **colloid** (e.g., pureed broccoli soup — particles collide and dissipate energy, i.e. **steric hindrance/resistance**); (2) dissolving long-chain polymer molecules (**hydrocolloids**) that tangle and increase viscosity; (3) activating raw materials already present in the food (e.g., heat-denatured egg or dairy proteins) to form a network without adding anything.
- Human mouths detect suspended particles down to **7–10 microns (3–4 ten-thousandths of an inch)**; below that, texture reads as smooth; at or above it, as grittiness.
- Reduction is a traditional thickening method: evaporating water concentrates suspended particles or dissolved solids (works for purees, emulsions like cream, and stocks becoming demiglace). Vacuum reduction (detailed in Vol. 2) thickens/concentrates without heat.
- "Thickening oil" can be done via emulsification (see Ch. 15) or via a starch like **N-Zorbit** (tapioca-derived, adsorbs oil so it can be turned into a powder — used for flavored oil powders).

### Starches
- Natural starches = amylose (linear, better at gelling) + amylopectin (branched, better at thickening/water-binding). Corn and wheat starch ≈ 25% amylose / 75% amylopectin; tapioca ≈ 18%/82%; waxy corn is nearly all amylopectin.
- Process: **dispersion** (distribute starch granules without clumping, typically as a cold slurry) then **hydration** (granules absorb water and swell — "gelatinization"). Wheat flour is often first coated in fat (roux) to prevent lumping.
- **Best Bets for Thickening with Natural Starch** (scaling = % of liquid weight; heat to ≥80°C/176°F to hydrate):
  - Arrowroot: broths 1%, jus/gravies 2% — clear, neutral, not heat-sensitive, unstable when thawed.
  - Corn: broths 1.5%, veloutés/cream sauces 2–2.5%, gravies 3–4%, puddings 5–6.5% — opaque, strong flavor, heat-sensitive.
  - Kudzu: broths 2%, glazes 5–7%, dense puddings/tofu 12% — clear, gelatinous, neutral.
  - Potato: broths 1%, jus/gravies 2%, stir-fry sauces 2.5% — clear, sticky.
  - Rice: glazes 3–3.5%, soups 4–5%, puddings/purees 7–8% — opaque, sticky.
  - Tapioca: broths 1.5–2%, jus/fillings 2.5–3%, fruit fillings 4–5% — clear, sticky, stable when thawed.
  - Waxy corn: broths 1%, dressings 2.5%, gravies 4% — opaque, creamy, stable when thawed.
  - Wheat: veloutés/cream sauces 2.5%, gravies 3–3.5%, béchamel 4–5% — opaque, strong flavor, heat-sensitive.
- Modified/prehydrated starch products (National Starch, GPC, General Mills) give "pour-and-stir" convenience:
  - **N-Zorbit M** (National Starch, tapioca): 20–100% scaling, nearly opaque, swelled-granule texture, unstable when heated/thawed — used for oil powders (30–40%) and oil pastes (85–100%).
  - **Ultra-Sperse 3** (National Starch, tapioca): 0.2–8%, nearly-to-fully opaque, smooth, stable when heated and thawed — meat gravies/jus 3–4%, puddings 6–8%.
  - **Flojel 60** (National Starch, corn): 1–10%, opaque, smooth/stringy — cream sauce 4%, puddings 7%.
  - **Novation Prima 600** (National Starch, corn): 1–7%, opaque, smooth — broths 2–2.5%, purees 4%; heat to 75°C/167°F to hydrate.
  - **Ultra-Tex 8** (National Starch, tapioca): 1–10%, nearly opaque, creamy — cream sauce 3–3.5%, gravies/jus 4%, purees 6%, puddings 8%.
  - **Pure-Cote B790** (GPC, corn): 0.5–20%, clear, smooth, stable heated/unstable thawed — films 12%, thickened juices 15%.
  - **Maltrin** (GPC, corn): 1–30%, clear, smooth — thin sauces 10%, purees 20%.
  - **Wondra** (General Mills, barley malt/wheat): 1–15%, opaque, smooth — gravies/veloutés 6–7%, béchamel 8–9%; heat to a simmer to hydrate.
- **Example recipe — Pregelatinized Starch Paste** (yields 500 g): water 50 g + tapioca starch 50 g whisked, vacuum sealed, cooked sous vide **80°C/176°F for 2 h**, then blended into 400 g hot water; strained/cooled/stored vacuum-sealed. Used as a substitute for the discontinued commercial product Micri (invented by chef/neurologist Miguel Sánchez Romera).
- **Example recipe — Modernist Béchamel** (yields 215 g): roux made by pressure-cooking 250 g butter + 250 g flour in jars at **1 bar/15 psi gauge pressure for 2 h**; 15 g of the cooked roux whisked into 200 g milk (warmed to 45°C/113°F) with 7 g Ultra-Sperse 3 + 0.2 g lambda carrageenan; warmed to serving temperature.
- **N-Zorbit example recipes**: Malt Vinegar Powder (spray-dried malt vinegar 100% + N-Zorbit M 4%); Bacon Powder Squares (bacon fat + 80% N-Zorbit M, formed into 13 mm squares wrapped in pineapple "glass"); Parmesan Nuggets (Adrià-inspired, Parmesan oil + 25% N-Zorbit M, sautéed into spheres); Vanilla Olive Oil Powder (Achatz-inspired); Almond Polenta and Corn Pebbles (Dufresne-inspired, baked at 135°C/275°F ~6 min); Edible Earth (Redzepi/Dufresne homage — almond flour, dehydrated black bread at 135°C/275°F, N-Zorbit M, porcini/shiitake powders).

### Hydrocolloids
- Dispersed cold, then hydrated by controlled heating (exceptions: cellulose gums like methylcellulose disperse best hot; xanthan/guar/gum arabic/some carrageenans hydrate without heat).
- Three dispersion techniques: direct dispersion in cold water with shear; dispersion in oil first (uncooked roux-style; xanthan good at oil:xanthan ≥3:1); premixing with a dry bulking solid (sugar:pectin ratio ≥5:1).
- **How to disperse a thickener**: sprinkle powder over liquid surface, blend at least **1 full minute** (rotor-stator homogenizer, magnetic stirrer, hand/commercial blender, or Thermomix — power setting 3 disperses in ~1 min). Overmixing (>a few minutes) can undermine performance.
- **How to hydrate**: warm dispersed solution and hold at hydration temperature for **2–3 min**; sous vide water-bath hydration is preferred for precision and to avoid evaporation-driven concentration changes.
- **Hydrocolloid Properties and Uses table** (selected): xanthan gum hydrates >90°C/194°F cold-tolerant, gels/melts n/a, scaling 0.05–0.8%, pH range 4–10, tolerates salt/alcohol; agar hydrates at 95°C/203°F, gels 35–45°C/95–113°F, melts 85°C/185°F, pH 2.5–10, tolerates salt/sugar/alcohol but shows extreme hysteresis; kappa carrageenan hydrates >70°C/158°F, gels 30–60°C/86–140°F, melts 40–75°C/104–167°F, prone to syneresis, brittle with potassium; iota carrageenan hydrates >70°C/158°F, gels 40–70°C/104–158°F, melts 50–80°C/122–176°F, elastic with calcium; gelatin hydrates cold, gels 4–35°C/39–95°F, melts 37°C/99°F (body temperature); LA gellan gels at pH ≥9, HA gellan gels 40–85°C/104–185°F; konjac gum requires cold+high shear, best hydrated >1 h.
- **Hydrocolloid Interactions (synergies)**: xanthan + locust bean gum = stronger gel than either alone; guar + xanthan = higher viscosity; agar + locust bean/guar/konjac gum = higher viscosity; CMC + agar = stronger gel.
- **Thickening Hot and Cold Liquids — Best Bets for Thickening Cold Liquids** (scaling = % of liquid): very little = 0.15% xanthan or 0.35% guar; little = 0.15% lambda carrageenan + 0.1% xanthan; moderate = 0.25% guar + 0.5% cellulose gum LV, or 0.4% xanthan; great = 0.3% konjac gum + 0.1% locust bean gum (shear 5 min, hydrate 82°C/180°F for 3 min); very great = 0.5% gum tragacanth + 0.2% xanthan, or 2.25% cellulose gum LV + 0.5% propylene glycol alginate.
- **Synersis (weeping)** prevention example: 0.2% xanthan gum + 0.5% Ultra-Sperse 3 stops weeping in gazpacho/tomato-based liquids.
- Proprietary blends: CP Kelco, TIC Gums, National Starch each make trade-secret formulations (e.g., Kelcogel LT100 gellan) — substituting between brands may require adjusting amount, temperature, or technique.

## Chapter 14 — Gels

### How gelling works
- A gel = a 3-D molecular network that traps liquid, formed when long polymer molecules link (directly or via a coagulant).
- **Thermo-reversible** gels (e.g., gelatin) melt above and reset below melting point without degrading; **thermo-irreversible** gels (e.g., egg white) do not return to liquid once set.
- Common coagulants: calcium (and other) ions for hydrocolloids; acid (H+ ions) for proteins (ricotta, fresh cheeses); enzymes (rennet/chymosin for cheese curds; **transglutaminase** — the active ingredient in Activa "meat glue" — links protein amino acids, can stiffen gelatin/egg/dairy gels and raise melting temperature while reducing syneresis).
- **Sequestrants** (sodium hexametaphosphate/SHMP, sodium citrate) bind free ions to delay/control gelling — critical for spherification and for hydrating ion-sensitive hydrocolloids at lower temperatures.
- Synergy: xanthan gum (thickens but won't gel alone) + locust bean gum (also won't gel alone) together form a solid gel.
- History aside: aerogels (invented 1931 by Samuel Stephens Kistler via supercritical drying) are the lowest-density solids ever created — same "trapped network" principle taken to its extreme.

### Egg gels
- Egg proteins are thermo-irreversible; below ~55°C/130°F they never gel even if held for hours. That threshold allows **pasteurizing eggs in the shell** by holding time-temperature combinations (raw texture/functionality preserved).
- Whole-egg thermal equilibrium in a water bath takes ~35 min (quail eggs faster, goose eggs to 40 min).
- **Two-step soft/pasteurized egg**: cook at 60°C/140°F for a liquid yolk or 64°C/147°F for a "more set" yolk; extend 12 min longer to pasteurize.
- **Egg Textures chart** (temperature, not time, determines texture):
  - 55°C/131°F — pasteurized 2 h: whole egg semiliquid, white runny, yolk runny.
  - 60°C/140°F — white starting to gel, yolk runny (this is the classic onsen tamago range, 62–65°C/144–149°F).
  - 62°C/144°F — onsen egg: white runny, yolk viscous.
  - 74°C/165°F — whole egg white+yolk set (their preferred "best bet" for whole egg); white just set; yolk just solid. This is their preferred hard-boiled-egg replacement temperature.
  - 78°C/172°F — medium-boiled/elastic, moderately firm white, moist yolk.
  - 80°C/176°F — hard-boiled, firm white, tender yolk.
- Reference points from other chefs: Aki Kamozawa/H. Alexander Talbot prefer 75°C/167°F for 13 min (chicken egg); Ferran Adrià uses 7 min at 70°C/158°F for quail eggs.
- "Fried" egg technique: cook white separately in rings in a steam oven at **80°C/175°F**, then add raw yolk and return to **60°C/140°F** until yolk sets — gives white and yolk each their individually ideal doneness.
- Egg gels can be turned into **fluid gels** by shearing a fully-set egg gel with a blender/homogenizer — instantly turns solid back to liquid (works only on gels not heated enough to fully set).

### Dairy/tofu/hydrocolloid gels & fluid gels
- Traditional gel classes: starch (cornstarch, flour, tapioca); protein (bean curd/tofu with calcium or magnesium chloride, meat-emulsion sausages, egg white/yolk/whole-egg gels, milk with rennet/acid); gelatin (stock-derived, fish gelatin, powdered extracts).
- **Fluid gels**: gels sheared to flow like a liquid at rest but that thicken/set when undisturbed; extremely good at suspending particles because of shear-thinning behavior. Example recipes: Everything Bagel Broth and Squid Ink Fluid Gel both use low-acyl gellan (Kelcogel F) + sodium hexametaphosphate, centrifuged at **27,500g for 1 h** to clarify.
- **Ion-coagulated gels** (require calcium/other ions): alginate, LA gellan (most ion-sensitive), iota carrageenan, LM pectin.
  - LA gellan hydration temperature depends on ion content: deionized water 75°C/167°F; soft tap water 88°C/190°F; hard tap water >100°C/212°F.
  - **Sources of Calcium** table: calcium chloride (anhydrous) 36.1% calcium content, 0.11% scaling to make a 0.04% calcium solution; calcium sulfate 29.4% content, 0.14% scaling; calcium lactate 18.4% content, 0.22% scaling; calcium gluconate 9.3% content, 0.43% scaling.
  - **Sequestrant table** (for a 0.3% LA gellan solution): milk/cream hydrate alone at 90°C/194°F, or with 0.15% sodium citrate/0.10% SHMP at 22°C/72°F; deionized water hydrates alone at 75°C/167°F or with sequestrant at 22°C/72°F; hard water (130–180 ppm calcium) needs 0.34% SC or 0.22% SHMP, hydrating at 58°C/136°F.
- Carrageenan types: kappa (brittle gels, effective on milk proteins <0.5%), iota (elastic with calcium), lambda (thickens only, does not gel alone).
- Agar: gels at up to 85°C/185°F or higher (highest-melting traditional gelling agent), historically from Asian cuisine, adopted heavily in France.
- **How to Cast Gels**: select serving vessel → disperse/hydrate → cast to thickness → set (varies by hydrocolloid, see properties table) → optional second layer/garnish. Variations: molding gels (silicone molds), molding with suspended solids (partial set then add solid then finish casting), casting thick sheets with pastry-bar molds, cutting "linguine" from thin sheets.

### Spherification
- Coined by Ferran Adrià; first used commercially/for fine dining in **2003** at elBulli.
- **Direct spherification**: alginate (1%) ± xanthan gum (0.2–0.5%) dropped into a calcium chloride (0.5%) or calcium gluconate (2.5%) bath; skin forms in 1–3 min; continues gelling over time (heat to 85°C/185°F for 10 min to stop the reaction).
- **Reverse spherification** (preferred method): calcium (e.g., calcium lactate 3%) is in the edible liquid; bath is dilute alginate (0.5%) or sodium alginate with sodium citrate (1.2%) for acidic/high-calcium liquids. Center stays permanently liquid since no hydrocolloid is present there. Beads store in water/oil/infusions up to 3 days.
- **Cryospherification** (molded spherification): freeze the liquid to be spherified, then drop frozen pieces into a hydrocolloid bath; as they thaw, the outer layer gels — gives precise control of shape/size.
- **Best Bets for Spherification table**: alginate direct (1% sodium alginate + 0.5% CaCl₂ bath); alginate reverse (3% calcium lactate sphere + 0.5% sodium alginate/1.2% sodium citrate bath); carrageenan direct (2% iota + 5% potassium phosphate bath, hydrate cold ≥5 h); gellan direct (0.2% low-acyl gellan + 0.1% SHMP, bath 6% calcium gluconate/0.1% lactic acid, make frozen hemispheres, set in 80°C/176°F bath); LM pectin reverse (5% calcium lactate + 2% LM pectin bath).
- Example recipes with real formulas: **Carbonated Mojito Spheres** (José Andrés) — lime/mint/rum base with 1.9% calcium lactate, 0.5% xanthan gum, spherified in a 1.43% sodium alginate bath, set 7 min, then charged with CO₂ cartridges in a whipping siphon, refrigerated ≥5 h to carbonate. **Melon Caviar** (Adrià, original 2003) — 0.8% sodium alginate in melon juice, dropped into 1% calcium chloride bath, 1 min set. **Gruyère Spheres** (Dufresne-inspired) — cheese emulsion with propylene glycol alginate (3%), dipped in LM pectin (8%) then calcium lactate (1%) bath, membrane forms in ≥90 s. **Liquid Pimento Olive** (Adrià, original 2005) — frozen olive-puree hemispheres (1% xanthan gum, 2.7% calcium lactate) dropped into a simmering 1% sodium alginate bath. **Prosciutto and Melon "Raw Egg"** and **"Poached" Egg** — faux eggs built from spherified yolk analogues in plastic eggshell molds with xanthan/locust-bean-gum egg-white analogues.

## Chapter 15 — Emulsions

### How emulsification works
- **Bancroft's rule** (Wilder Dwight Bancroft, 1913): the phase in which the emulsifier is more soluble becomes the continuous phase. O/W emulsifiers are more water-soluble; W/O emulsifiers more oil-soluble. Rule of thumb: mix emulsifier into the bulk (continuous) phase first, then add dispersed phase while blending (this is why mayonnaise mixes lemon juice + egg yolk first, then beats in oil).
- **HLB (hydrophilic-lipophilic balance)** scale 0–20: antifoaming agents 0–3 (oleic acid 1.0); W/O emulsifiers 4–6 (mono/diglycerides, sorbitan monostearate 4.7); wetting agents 7–9; O/W emulsifiers 8–18 (soy lecithin 8.0, polysorbate 60/Tween 60 = 14.9, sucrose monolaurate 15.0); detergents 13–15; solubilizers 10–18.
- **Common Emulsions table**: beurre blanc (casein, fragile), butter (casein, less fragile), chocolate (lecithin/casein, ganache also uses polyglycerol polyricinoleate), Mountain Dew/sodas (gum arabic), espresso crema (coffee polysaccharides), hollandaise/mayonnaise (lecithin from egg yolk), ice cream (casein+egg yolk+fat droplets), milk/cream (casein), vinaigrette (mustard polysaccharides/proteins, fragile).
- Rule of thumb: O/W emulsifier volume should be about **5% of the oil-phase volume** (many exceptions).
- **Optics**: emulsions are opaque due to **Mie scattering** at the oil/water interface (index-of-refraction mismatch); microemulsions (droplets 5–50 nm) are transparent; nanoemulsions (20–100 nm, e.g. flavor oils in Mountain Dew via gum arabic) are thermodynamically unstable but practically long-lived.
- **Ouzo/pastis effect**: anise liqueurs are clear O/W microemulsions in the bottle; adding water disrupts the alcohol-mediated balance, driving droplets to a stable **1–1.5 micron (0.0004–0.0006 in)** size via Ostwald ripening — turns milky and stays that way (mechanism confirmed to be Ostwald ripening, not coalescence).
- Blood is ~3x more viscous than water due to colloidal blood cells — analogous to how emulsion viscosity rises with dispersed-phase concentration (mayonnaise can be thick enough to stand a knife upright).
- Most food emulsions break if heated above **70°C/158°F** (many exceptions).

### Methods of emulsifying
- Two stages: **primary emulsion** (initial coarse dispersion) and **secondary emulsion** (further-reduced droplet size, more stable, longer shelf life).
- Droplet sizes by tool: whisk = coarse, unstable; blender ≈ 10 microns/0.0004 in; food processor slightly coarser than blender; rotor-stator homogenizer reduces most droplets to **~3 microns** (blades as close as 25 microns/0.001 in apart); high-pressure homogenizer produces droplets averaging **<0.1 micron**, the finest achievable, used only for secondary emulsions.
- **Tools for Making Emulsions table**: handheld whisk (low power, light mayo/vinaigrette, large unstable droplets); electric whisk (medium); hand blender (medium, beurre blanc/hollandaise); household blender (medium); commercial blender (high); rotor-stator homogenizer (very high — "our favorite tool," restructured cream, ultrastable beurre blanc, microemulsion); ultrasonic homogenizer (very high, best for long-term-stable secondary emulsions); high-pressure homogenizer (extremely high, ultrastable ice cream/restructured cream).
- Phase inversion: cream (O/W) becomes butter (W/O) via mechanical agitation + suitable temperature — a rare traditional example of controlled phase inversion.

### How emulsions fail
- **Creaming** (O/W, oil rises), **sedimentation** (W/O, water sinks), **flocculation** (droplets clump into complexes without merging), **coalescence** (droplets merge into larger ones), **Ostwald ripening** (small droplets shrink, feeding larger ones, via molecular diffusion through the continuous phase — occurs even in liqueur microemulsions).
- Weighting agents (e.g., brominated vegetable oil) increase oil density to slow separation — used, e.g., in the Squid Ink Fluid Gel dish (Ch. 14) to keep black-pepper oil droplets suspended in a fluid-gel consommé.

## Chapter 16 — Foams

### How foams work
- Foaming agents fall into six categories: fats, proteins, gelling agents, starches, surfactants, and solids.
- **Set foams** are stabilized by heat-set (thermo-irreversible) proteins — e.g., soufflé, sabayon, caffè latte foam (steam denatures milk proteins). Counterintuitively, fat — which stabilizes whipped cream — actually undermines protein-stabilized foams (fat blocks proteins from coating bubbles), explaining why nonfat milk foams better and more durably for espresso drinks than whole milk.
- Cream whips finer and holds longer with a **hot-cold treatment**: heat cream to 30°C/86°F, hold 30 min, then chill to 5°C/41°F before whipping (anneals fat-crystal structure, similar to chocolate tempering).
- **Foam Geometry**: Kelvin's 1887 "Kelvin Problem" (minimal-surface-area partition of space) was solved with a 14-sided tetradecahedron; in 1993 Weaire and Phelan found a structure requiring 0.3% less surface area, later used as the design basis for Beijing's "Water Cube" (2008 Olympics).
- **Traditional Foams table**: fat foam (whipped cream/parfait, milk fat ± gelatin, air/N₂O, whisk/siphon); starch foam (baked goods flour, puffed snacks tapioca, CO₂/steam); egg foam (sabayon egg-yolk gel, meringue egg-white gel, soufflé mixed-egg gel, air/steam); other protein foam (caffè latte foam via steam wand, mousse via gelatin+whisk).
- **Rise and fall of a soufflé**: initial dense foam of whipped egg whites folded into a starch-containing yolk base; bubbles enlarge as hot air expands and water vaporizes; starch/protein gels set in the bubble walls as heat progresses; larger bubbles = less stable soufflé.
- **Foams vs. emulsions**: foam bubbles (fine foam 0.01–0.1 mm; coarse foam several mm) are 10–100x larger than typical emulsion droplets (0.001–0.01 mm). Foams die faster than emulsions via **draining** (analogous to creaming/sedimentation) and Ostwald ripening (gas is far more soluble in water than oil is, accelerating bubble dissolution/growth through thin films).
- **Guinness widget**: nitrogen (much less soluble than CO₂) is injected via a floating plastic "widget" that releases gas when the can/bottle is opened, mimicking a draft-style creamy head; the 2006 "Surger" uses ultrasound-driven cavitation instead.
- **Traditional Foaming Agents table**: egg white — albumin, 8–100% typical concentration, meringue/soufflé/sponge cake (one egg white = 12.5% albumin); egg yolk — lecithin, 3–30%, hollandaise/sabayon; butter — milk protein+milkfat, 1–75%, whipped butter (stable emulsion 46–56°C/115–133°F); soy — lecithin, 0.5–2.5%; gelatin — 0.2–2.0%, cold foams only unless transglutaminase-stabilized; milk — casein+whey, 5–100%; dairy — casein+whey, 2–100%.
- **Forming foams**: whisk works via air entrainment + shear-force bubble refinement; blenders and rotor-stator/high-pressure/ultrasonic homogenizers are all poor at foaming (blades submerged, no air intake) despite excelling at emulsions. **Vacuum inflation** expands dissolved/existing gas by depressurizing (used industrially for vacuum-set foamed bread and can create frozen set foams from ice cream or melted chocolate under vacuum). Deep-frying creates a **steam foam** as liquid water flashes to vapor, expanding roughly **1,700-fold** in volume (mechanism behind pork cracklings, prawn crackers, pommes soufflées).
- Whipping-siphon foaming gas is usually **nitrous oxide (N₂O)**: a standard charger holds 8 g N₂O; about 2 g N₂O dissolves in a 1 L siphon of water at 4°C/39°F and 1 bar/14.7 psi (roughly 5x more dissolves at 5 bar/75 psi); N₂O is 5–6x more soluble in fat/oil than in water, making cream (≈36% fat) especially easy to aerate. CO₂ can also charge a siphon but leaves a characteristic acidic taste and dissolves more slowly, producing lingering fizz.
- **Modernist Foams table** (type/stabilizer/method/gas/example): fat-based foam (milk fat+hydrocolloid gel, siphon, N₂O, hot whipped cream — add agar/gellan for stability while hot); starch foam (potato starch, siphon, N₂O, baked potato foam); egg foam (egg yolk+stabilizers, siphon, N₂O, siphoned sabayon; egg white+stabilizers, siphon, instant Swiss meringue); sugar-glass foam (chemical leavening, vacuum, honeycomb/prune coals); fluid-gel foam (agar or low-acyl gellan fluid gel, siphon, N₂O, hot whipped cream / cauliflower foam); methylcellulose foam (methylcellulose+xanthan, electric whisk, air, hot marshmallow).
- **Foam Stabilizers table**: agar 0.2–1.2% (hot whipped cream); carrageenan 0.1–1.0% (cheese/milk foams); locust bean gum 0.1–1.0% (milk shake foam); low-acyl gellan 0.05–0.80% (hot cauliflower foam); modified starches 0.5–4.0%; propylene glycol alginate 0.1–0.4% (hollandaise, whipped cream); sodium alginate 0.1–1.2% (beer foam, whipped topping); sugar solids 2–30%; xanthan gum 0.1–0.7% (light foam).
- **Foam Inhibitors table**: acid (pH 2–5, can stabilize protein foam at low pH but usually inhibits); alcohol (>30 proof/15% ABV can dissolve/coagulate proteins); antifoaming agents (liquid glycerides HLB 0–2, polydimethylsiloxane in diet soda, silicone oil in frying oil); fat (egg whites can whip to 8x volume without fat present, only 3x with fat present).

## Chapter 17 — Wine

- **Terroir debate**: a 2005 Royal Economic Society study (French/Belgian authors) concluded terroir plays no measurable role in great-wine production; winemaking technology and skilled enology were found to be the determining factors. Famous plots are often small (La Romanée-Conti < 2 hectares/4.5 acres) yet run along arbitrary rectangular property lines, undermining strict soil-determinism.
- **The Bordeaux Equation** (Orley Ashenfelter, Princeton economist): a formula using winter rainfall, average growing-season temperature, and harvest rainfall predicts about **82%** of the variation in a Bordeaux vintage's 10-year auction price (based on 1952–2003 data); wine critic Robert Parker called it "an absolute total sham." In most regions, simple weather correlations explain **50–80%** of year-to-year quality variation; all other factors (terroir, microclimate, clonal variation, winemaker) together account for at most ~20%.
- **Grape genetics**: ~90% of world grape production is *Vitis vinifera*, propagated almost entirely by grafting/cloning rather than seed (over 10,000 cultivars exist; ~230 are commercially significant). All Chardonnay descends from a single primordial vine (a spontaneous Gouais Blanc × Pinot Noir cross, likely >1,000 years old); all white grapes trace to a rare set of gene mutations in one vine giving rise to >55 white cultivars. Zinfandel was DNA-matched in 1994 to Italy's Primitivo, and in 2001 to Croatia's ancient Crljenak Kaštelanski variety. Phylloxera nearly destroyed European *V. vinifera* in the late 19th century; grafting onto resistant American rootstocks (*Vitis labrusca*) saved it and remains the standard defense today.
- **Modern winemaking technology**: cryoconcentration (freeze concentration, cousin to ice wine), vacuum evaporation, reverse osmosis (removes water without removing anything else), chaptalization (adding sugar pre-fermentation, legal in France, illegal in California), microoxygenation/MOX (controlled oxygen dosing to soften tannins), dealcoholization (removes alcohol post-fermentation but changes structure/character), nanofiltration (removes large off-flavor molecules).
- **Regulatory systems**: France's AOC (Appellation d'Origine Contrôlée, established mid-1930s, overseen by INAO) regulates region, grape varieties, yields, and technique; Italy's DOC (1963) and superior DOCG (1980, e.g. Brunello, Barolo, Barbaresco) parallel it. Napoleon III's 1855 Bordeaux classification (premier/deuxième/... grand cru classé) remains essentially unchanged 150+ years later except for Mouton-Rothschild's 1973 promotion to first-growth.
- **Robert Parker**: created the 100-point wine scale in his newsletter *The Wine Advocate* (subscription since 1984, launched free in 1978); erobertparker.com since 2002. "Parkerization" describes winemakers crafting wine to suit his palate specifically.
- **Tasting wine**: the **triangle test** (two identical samples + one different) is the standard method for detecting a perceptible difference between wines — cited as useful for winemakers testing small production variations (e.g., a 5°C difference in bottle-aging temperature). A 2001 study by cognitive psychologist Frédéric Brochet (University of Bordeaux) used odorless red dye to tint white wine red; a panel of 54 expert tasters described the fake "red" using red-wine vocabulary — demonstrating how heavily wine perception depends on visual/contextual cues.

## Chapter 18 — Coffee

### Brewing fundamentals
- Extraction methods: **infusion** (steeping, e.g. French press), **percolation** (water passing through grounds), **decoction** (boiling, e.g. Turkish coffee).
- Target extraction: **18–22%** of soluble materials by weight from the ground bean. Below 18% = sour/underextracted; above 22% = bitter/overextracted. Strength (solubles concentration) is independently controlled by the coffee-to-water ratio, ideally **1.15–1.40%** solubles concentration in the cup ("golden cup" zone on the brewing control chart, extraction 18–22% × strength 1.15–1.40%).
- Brewing control chart tools: a scale (weigh dose and water), a TDS meter (ppm or %), and the chart itself. Adjusting variables: finer grind → more bitter/stronger; coarser grind → more sour, somewhat weaker; more dose → stronger with little effect on extraction; longer brew time → moves toward stronger/less sour; shorter time → weaker/less bitter.
- **Cupping protocol**: grind finer than French press, coarser than drip; ~**55 g of grounds per liter of water** (about 9.5 g for a 175 mL/6 oz cup); add hot water, steep 5 min, let crust form; break crust and evaluate aroma; steep 7–8 more minutes; slurp loudly to spray coffee across the palate.
- General brewing water temperature: **91–96°C/196–205°F**, with the authors' preference at **93°C/199°F**.
- Standard drip starting ratio: **55 g/1.9 oz grounds per 1,000 g/35.3 oz water**, adjusted to taste; let coffee "bloom" for **30–45 s** after initial wetting before continuing to pour, for even extraction.
- **French press**: coarse grounds, steep 30–45 s before stirring, then wait **4 min** before plunging.
- **AeroPress**: invented 2005 by Alan Adler; recommended water temperature **80°C/176°F**; ~20 g finely ground coffee, stir 10–12 seconds, plunge slowly.
- **Vacuum brewer**: medium-coarse grind, ~2 min brew timer once water rises to the upper chamber.
- **Cold extraction (Toddy-style)**: ratio **225 g/8 oz medium-grind coffee per 1,000 g/35.3 oz ice-cold water**, steeped **12 h** refrigerated; concentrate keeps refrigerated up to 2 weeks; diluted 3 parts hot water to 1 part concentrate for serving (other ratios give espresso-like strength or, with hot milk, café au lait).
- Cooking with coffee: coffee-in-fat extraction (e.g., Coffee Butter — whole roasted beans + butter, sous vide **90°C/194°F for 4 h**, or boiled 4 h) captures aroma without over-extracting bitterness; espresso itself is not recommended as a cooking ingredient because its volatile compounds dissipate too fast once off the machine.

### Espresso
- Invented **1901** by Luigi Bezzera (Milan); patent purchased **1905** by Desiderio Pavoni; the name means "fast" — espresso was one of the first "fast foods."
- Key variables per shot: gauge pressure **8–10 bar (116–145 psi)**; water temperature **85–96°C/185–205°F**; dose of ground coffee **7–21 g/¼–¾ oz**; extraction duration **18–35 s**; output volume **15–45 mL/½–1½ oz**. The authors' working standard is **9 bar/130 psi** gauge pressure and **95°C/203°F** water, adjusted per bean/roast.
- **Single/Double/Triple Shot table** (grounds → shot volume → shot weight → brew ratio):
  - *Ristretto*: single 7 g → 15 mL/0.5 oz → 7 g (ratio 1:1); double 14 g → 30 mL/1 oz → 14 g; triple 21 g → 45 mL/1.5 oz → 21 g.
  - *Normale*: single 7 g → 30 mL/1 oz → 14 g (ratio 2:1); double 14 g → 60 mL/2 oz → 28 g; triple 21 g → 90 mL/3 oz → 42 g.
  - *Lungo*: single 7 g → 45 mL/1.5 oz → 21 g (ratio 3:1); double 14 g → 90 mL/3 oz → 42 g; triple 21 g → 135 mL/4.5 oz → 63 g.
  - *Caffè crema*: single 7 g → 105 mL/3.5 oz → 49 g (ratio 7:1); double 14 g → 210 mL/7 oz → 98 g; triple 21 g → 315 mL/10 oz → 147 g.
  - Standard Italian *normale* single dose = 7 g/¼ oz ground coffee → 14 g/½ oz espresso in the cup (2:1). Many modern shops now pull a "double ristretto" as their standard single (14 g dose → 14 g/½ oz out), using twice the traditional bean weight for the same cup volume.
- **The grind**: espresso is far more grind-sensitive than drip/press coffee because contact time is only 18–35 s vs. 3–4 min (or 12 h for cold brew) for other methods. Burr grinders (conical or flat) are strongly preferred over blade grinders for particle-size uniformity; burrs dull over time and should be refurbished; heat buildup during heavy grinding changes particle size, so some machines (pioneered by David Schomer of Espresso Vivace) add cooling fans to grinders.
- **Dosing/grooming/tamping**: consistency to within about half a gram of target dose is considered critical; uneven distribution in the portafilter causes uneven (channeled) extraction and inconsistent flavor.
- Blends are typical (rather than single-origin) because espresso extraction amplifies bean character strongly, for better and worse, so roasters blend beans/roast levels to balance aroma, acidity, richness and body — analogous to wine blending.
- Espresso in Italy is culturally routine (price is legally regulated for coffee consumed standing at the bar) yet quality is inconsistent even there; the authors compare Sant'Eustachio Il Caffè (Rome) with Espresso Vivace (Seattle) as benchmark examples of excellence achieved via opposite philosophies (secrecy vs. openly publishing pressure/temperature settings).
