## 1. VISCOSITY FUNDAMENTALS

- **Viscosity** = a fluid's resistance to shear forces; measured in **centipoise (cP)** or Pascal-seconds (Pa·s = cP ÷ 1,000).
- Reference fluid: **water = 1 cP at 20 °C / 68 °F**.
- Water's viscosity is strongly temperature-dependent: **1.8 cP at 0 °C/32 °F**, drops to **0.5 cP at 60 °C/140 °F**, and **0.3 cP just below boiling** — a 6.4× range across its liquid temperature span. This is why body/mouthfeel for a consommé served hot can drop by more than half versus room temperature.
- **Newtonian fluids**: flow rate directly proportional to force applied (water, milk, cream, oils, corn syrup, honey, molasses, chocolate syrup, liquid nitrogen).
- **Non-Newtonian fluids**: viscosity changes with applied force.
  - **Shear-thinning** (e.g., ketchup, yogurt/thixotropic, tomato paste, peanut butter/plastic): resist initial force, flow more easily once moving.
  - **Shear-thickening** (e.g., puffed cracker dough): the opposite.
  - **Plastic fluids** (tomato paste, peanut butter, lard): behave like solids until a yield stress is exceeded.
- **Detectable particle size in the mouth**: 7–10 ten-thousandths of an inch (≈ 7–10 microns). Below this, particles read as smooth; at/above, as gritty.

## 5. STARCH SCIENCE (p.20–22)

- Two polymer types in natural starch: **amylose** (linear chains; better for gelling) and **amylopectin** (branched; better at water-binding/thickening).
- Typical composition: **corn and wheat starch ≈ 25% amylose / 75% amylopectin**; **tapioca starch ≈ 18% amylose / 82% amylopectin**. Waxy corn (discovered early 1900s China) is almost all amylopectin.
- Corn/rice starch granules are small → smoother cooked appearance; potato starch granules are large → grainier, mottled texture.
- **Dispersion** = evenly distributing starch granules before hydration (prevents "fish eyes"/lumps). Cold water preferred (slower absorption = more working time). Wheat flour forms lumps easily even in cold water and carries raw flavor — hence the classical **roux** (fat-coating of granules) to prevent clumping and hide raw taste.
- Roux cooking levels: **blond** (lightly cooked, just removes raw taste), **medium/dark** (longer cooked for color/flavor; less thickening power, but flavor — not thickness — is the primary reason to darken it).
- **Gelatinization**: heat + water break the crystalline bonds in starch, allowing water penetration; the starch converts from ordered/crystalline to disordered/amorphous, and the liquid thickens or gels.
  - Example: **potato starch begins gelatinizing at 58 °C/136 °F**, continuing up to **65 °C/149 °F**.
  - Modified/"instant" starches (e.g., Ultra-Sperse 3, Ultra-Tex 8, Wondra) are pre-gelatinized by the manufacturer and thicken without further cooking/heat.
- **How To Disperse a Thickener** (4-step method, p.24):
  1. Bring liquid to correct temperature (most — not all — thickeners disperse best cold).
  2. Sprinkle powder evenly over the surface (mix multiple dry powders together before adding).
  3. Blend for **at least one full minute** for even distribution; avoid overmixing, which can undermine some thickeners' performance.
  4. If using a heat-capable blending tool (e.g., Thermomix), can proceed directly to hydration without transferring vessels.
- Cellulose gums (e.g., methylcellulose) are the notable exception: they disperse best in **hot** water, not cold.

## 6. EMULSIONS — FUNDAMENTALS (p.199–200)

- Definitions: **continuous (bulk) phase** = the phase that surrounds; **dispersed phase** = droplets suspended within it.
- **O/W (oil-in-water)**: continuous phase is water, dispersed phase is oil — e.g., milk, cream, mayonnaise.
- **W/O (water-in-oil)**: continuous phase is oil, dispersed phase is water — e.g., butter (butterfat continuous, with some crystallized fat stabilizing the water droplets).
- Rarer types: double emulsions **O/W/O** and **W/O/W** (droplets nested within droplets within a continuous phase) — used in cosmetics, not yet common in food.
- Emulsions are inherently **meta-stable**: given enough time they separate into component liquids.
- A droplet's surfactant coating is called a **micelle**.

## 8. BANCROFT'S RULE & OIL CONTENT BY EMULSION (p.202)

Bancroft's Rule (Wilder Dwight Bancroft, 1913): the nature of an emulsion (O/W vs W/O) is primarily determined by whether its emulsifier is more soluble in oil or in water. Water-soluble emulsifier → O/W; oil-soluble emulsifier → W/O. Mixing order matters: mix emulsifier into the phase it's most soluble in first, then add the dispersed phase while blending (this is why traditional mayonnaise mixes lecithin-carrying egg yolk into lemon juice first, then beats in oil).

**Oil content of common O/W emulsions (illustrates rule's limits since ratio alone doesn't determine type):**
| Emulsion | Oil (fat) content |
|---|---|
| milk | ~3.25% oil (milk fat) by weight |
| heavy whipping cream | ~36% oil |
| mayonnaise | typically >80% oil |
| butter | ≥80% oil, yet is W/O (not O/W) |

## 10. MICROEMULSIONS AND NANOEMULSIONS (p.205)

| Type | Droplet size | Stability | Optical appearance |
|---|---|---|---|
| macroemulsion (most food emulsions) | 0.1–100 microns (µm) | thermodynamically unstable (but kinetically stable enough to serve) | opaque/turbid |
| nanoemulsion | 20–100 nanometers (0.02–0.1 µm) | thermodynamically unstable but can last long enough | transparent |

- Nanoemulsion example: Mountain Dew and other sodas use gum arabic to nanoemulsify flavor oils.
- Microemulsions are not common in food but are used to encapsulate oil-soluble vitamins in some nutritional products.

## 11. OPTICS OF EMULSIONS (p.203)

- **Mie scattering**: large particles relative to light wavelength scatter all colors equally → opaque/white appearance (fog, clouds, cream, milk with added fat, glass beads).
- **Rayleigh scattering (Tyndall effect)**: very small particles comparable in size to light wavelength scatter some colors preferentially → bluish cast (nonfat milk, the sky).
- Cream: high fat-droplet concentration → strong Mie scattering → very white. Nonfat milk: fat mostly removed → faint Rayleigh/Tyndall bluish tint from milk protein particles.

## 12. METHODS AND TOOLS OF EMULSIFYING (p.206, 208)

- **Primary emulsion**: first-stage dispersal of one phase into another — coarse droplets.
- **Secondary emulsion**: further mechanical reduction of droplet size in an existing emulsion — finer, more stable, longer-lasting.
- Droplet size / gap benchmarks:
  - Standard blender: emulsion droplets down to about **10 microns / 0.0004 in**.
  - Rotor-stator homogenizer: blade gap as close as **25 microns / 0.001 in**; reduces most droplets to about **3 microns** diameter.
  - High-pressure homogenizer: finest droplets, **averaging less than 0.1 micron**; can be run multiple passes for further reduction; usable only for secondary emulsions.
  - Ultrasonic homogenizer: best for creating a fine **secondary** emulsion via mechanical vibration/probe; higher overheating risk, force concentrated near the probe tip.
- General rule: more emulsifier + smaller droplets = more stable, longer-lasting emulsion (with exceptions).
- Overheating risk: friction from mechanical emulsifying generates heat; mitigate by starting with very cold ingredients, using ice for part of the water phase, or processing in bursts with cooling breaks.
- **Phase inversion**: with the right mechanical agitation and temperature, an O/W emulsion (cream) can invert to a W/O emulsion (butter) — a classic example of controlled phase inversion in traditional cooking.

## 13. HOW EMULSIONS FAIL (p.210–212)

| Failure mode | Mechanism | Occurs in |
|---|---|---|
| **Creaming** | oil phase (lower density) floats to the surface | O/W emulsions only |
| **Sedimentation** | dispersed water droplets (heavier than oil) sink | W/O emulsions (equivalent of creaming) |
| **Flocculation** | droplets clump due to small electrostatic charge, chemical reactions, or excessive heating, forming "flocculation complexes"; increases creaming risk | any |
| **Coalescence** | droplets merge into larger droplets, reducing stability as size grows | any |
| **Ostwald ripening** | small droplets lose material (oil, or alcohol-solubilized components) to larger droplets over time via dissolution/redeposition at the droplet surface, so big droplets grow at the expense of small ones; the process continues until full separation | rare in cooking except cream liqueurs, ouzo/anise liqueurs, or other alcohol-containing microemulsions |

- Most food emulsions **break if heated above 70 °C / 158 °F**, with many exceptions.
- A **centrifuge** accelerates creaming/sedimentation by magnifying gravitational force by thousands of times — used deliberately to break emulsions (e.g., clarifying stock by removing fat) or to separate phases (e.g., breaking milk to make nonfat milk).
- Certain filtration techniques can also separate emulsion phases.

## 14. THICKENING OIL (p.13)

- Most thickening addresses water-based solutions; thickening **oil** itself requires different approaches:
  - Emulsify the oil with water or another liquid (an emulsion is inherently thicker than either pure phase — e.g., mayonnaise).
  - Use a thickener that works **directly on oil** without needing an emulsion first — e.g., **N-Zorbit** (tapioca-derived) adsorbs oil, turning it from thin liquid to slurry to solid mass depending on dose; used to convert oils to powder form for products like boxed cake mixes.
  - Mix an oil with another oil/fat of a higher melting point (e.g., blending deodorized, flavorless cocoa butter into olive oil yields an olive-oil-flavored fat that stays **solid at room temperature and remains "quite thick" even at 40 °C / 104 °F**).
